Start from the board of Chapter 8

Before anything else, do not start a new project. Instead, make a copy of the motor-driver project you finished in Chapter 8, open the copy, and work in that. In this chapter, you will extend the capstone board, not rebuild it.

The main learning objective in this chapter is reuse. You already have a working driver stage. It contains a DRV8833 with its three bypass capacitors, its sense-resistor option, and its motor terminals. What you are about to do is capture that stage as a reusable object and stamp out a second one, exactly as you stamped out three more matrix cells in Chapter 7.

There is an additional benefit in following this process. Once the second stage exists, you can mark it ‘do not populate’ so that the same project produces the two-motor board again. If, later, you want to add the second stage, the board already has the footprints to receive the parts. One project, two boards, one bill of materials for each. That is the design-variant idea from Chapter 5, applied to a whole circuit block.

Learning objectives of this extension

This is an extension and not a new project, so most of the board is Chapter 8’s, built once and then reused. Three things here are new:

  1. Design blocks that carry copper. Chapter 7 captured a circuit and stamped it out three more times. The block you save here holds the circuit and the layout that implements it, so the second stage arrives placed and routed instead of only wired. Capturing that second half is the one command in this chapter you have not seen before.
  2. Component classes. A component class is a name you attach to a group of parts so that you can refer to the whole group by that one name. Chapter 3, Board Setup, introduced them on this board in its section on component classes and said the two-motor version could do without them. You need one here for the last section. A class that names the eight parts of the second stage lets you mark all eight do-not-populate in a single step, which is what turns the four-motor board back into a two-motor one, while keeping the ability to add the second stage open.
  3. A design variant applied to a whole block. Chapter 5 varied a pair of resistors. Here the override covers a complete captured stage, and one project produces both the four-motor board and Chapter 8’s two-motor board.

Of course, reuse is not risk-free. The part, the footprint and the custom rules all propagate through the copy, which is why you must take care to set them up properly. Despite the fact that the first stage is properly setup, there are still two things we will need to fix: the Power class width, which was originally set for a single chip’s 3 A (and now facing 6 A), and the breakout rule’s scope. That rule is Chapter 8’s “U1 fine-pitch breakout”, which relaxes track width and clearance so that the narrow escapes from the fine-pitch package pass the design rules check. Its scope is a single named rule area drawn around U1, so it stops covering the second chip when that chip is added to the board.

The four-motor board

The extension converts the capstone project board into a four-motor driver built from two DRV8833PWP chips. This board is sufficient for a small four-wheeled robot with independent control of each wheel. The architecture is simple so you can focus on the build:

  • Two identical driver stages. Each is one DRV8833PWP with its own local decoupling (10 µF on VM, 2.2 µF on VINT, 0.01 µF on VCP), motor terminals, and pair of sense resistors. Decoupling stays per-chip and close to its chip.
  • One shared motor supply. A single VM input feeds both stages through one bulk capacitor near the connector, with each chip’s own 10 µF ceramic doing the local high-frequency work. The VM rail now carries both chips’ motor current. In Chapter 8 we sized the Power class for 3 A, but in this extension, having to supply four bridges, the maximum current is 6 A. Widening the VM rail will be the first job of this chapter’s board setup.
  • Eight logic inputs, one enable, one fault. Four motors need eight direction inputs. To keep the MCU pin count low, the two nSLEEP pins tie together as a single board enable, and the two nFAULT pins, being open-drain, wire together onto one shared pull-up, so a fault on either chip pulls the common line low.
  • One ground. A common ground pour, with both GND pins and both exposed pads tied to it, each over its own field of thermal vias.

Stage one keeps every name Chapter 8 gave it and drives motors A and B. Stage two continues with the naming pattern so that U2 drives motors C and D through J5 and J6, its sense resistors are R6 and R7, and its logic nets are CIN1, CIN2, DIN1 and DIN2. Remember that a design block reproduces its labels verbatim, so every one of those names can only be changed after the stamping. The section on placing the second stage is mostly about which names change.

Capturing the driver stage as a design block

You met design blocks in Chapter 7, where one cell of the LED matrix was drawn, saved into a library, and then stamped out three more times. We will follow the same process here.

We will capture the driver stage exactly as Chapter 8 left it. The driver was placed to typed coordinates, routed in copper of three different widths, and proven by a design rules check and no outstanding work. That is the more common situation in real work, because the circuit you want to reuse is nearly always one that somebody has already taken as far as a board.

A design block can be saved in two forms. Saved from the schematic alone, it holds the circuit and nothing else, so every copy you stamp arrives wired but unplaced. Saved from the schematic and from the board, it holds the circuit together with the copper that implements it, so the second stage arrives placed and routed. This chapter uses the second form.

Eight parts are included in the block:

  • U1, the DRV8833.
  • C2, C3 and C4, the three bypass capacitors, each sitting over the supply pin it serves.
  • R2 and R3, the two sense resistors.
  • J2 and J3, the two motor terminals.

Everything else on the board stays outside. To decide which parts should be part of the block, ask whether a second chip would need one of its own. A second DRV8833 needs its own decoupling, its own sense resistors and its own terminals, so those go in. But it does not need a second supply connector, reverse-polarity MOSFET, bulk capacitor, power indicator or control header, which is why J1, Q1, C1, R4, D1, R5 and J4 all stay where they are. The four mounting holes stay out for the same reason: they belong to the board rather than to a stage.

R1 needs more thinking, because it is a resistor sitting on a net that belongs to the stage. It is the nFAULT pull-up, and Chapter 8 put it beside the control header rather than beside the chip on purpose. nFAULT is an open-drain output, which can pull its line down but never drive it up, so two chips are able to share one line and one resistor between them. If you copy R1 into the block, then the four-motor board gets two pull-ups on one net, which halves the resistance and achieves nothing else. Chapter 8 kept it outside from the first placement so that this section would not have to take it back out.

Make the library before you save anything. A design block lives in a library, similarly to how a symbol or a footprint are included in libraries. Open the panel with “View”, then “Panels”, then “Design Blocks”, right-click anywhere in its library tree and choose “New Library…”. I called mine klp5e-ch09-blocks and added it to the project library table, which keeps this chapter’s files together with the project you copied at the start.

For this block, you could consider adding the new library in the global projects table. Chapter 7’s block was one cell of one particular matrix board, useful nowhere else. But the one we are working on here is a complete DRV8833 stage, and if you ever build another board around this chip, the block is what you will want to reuse. If that is your situation, put it in a global library instead and nothing else in the section changes. Remember what Chapter 8 said about ${KIPRJMOD} when it asked you to copy the project: a library kept with the project travels with it, but a global one does not.

A design block library is a folder rather than a file, and its name carries the suffix .kicad_blocks. Inside it, each block is a folder of its own holding a .kicad_sch file and, once you have done the second half of this section, there will also be a .kicad_pcb file beside it. There is nothing proprietary in either of them. If you want to see what you have captured, open the folder and read them.

Go ahead to capture the schematic half. Drag a selection box around the eight parts, taking in the wires, the labels and the ground symbols with them. Chapter 8’s placement was arranged to make this drag possible. The stage was drawn as one cluster on the sheet with clear space around it, and everything that exists only once was put out on the left, so that a single rectangle catches all of what should be in and none of what should not.

Check the edges of that rectangle before you continue. The ground symbols on R2, R3 and on U1’s pins 13 and 17 are inside the stage and belong in the block. R1 and J4 sit further off to the left. With your mouse, draw a box to enclose those items.

Then right-click klp5e-ch09-blocks in the panel and choose “Save Selection as Design Block…”. Three fields open with it:

  • Name: DRV8833-Stage.
  • Keywords: something you will search for later, such as DRV8833 h-bridge motor stage.
  • Description: this is the field to spend a minute on, because it is where you record the limitation. Mine reads “One DRV8833PWP with its VM, VINT and VCP decoupling, two 0.20 Ω sense resistors and two 2-pin screw terminals. Labels are stage A’s; rename the copy after placing it.”

When saving a block, KiCad groups the selected objects and fills in the new group’s “Library link” field so that it points back at the block, in the form klp5e-ch09-blocks:DRV8833-Stage. The stage you have just captured is therefore a linked group on the sheet, without any further work: a group that carries a pointer back to the block it came from. That link is what makes several of the operations in the next section possible.

Now capture the layout half, into the same block. Switch to the PCB Editor and open the Design Blocks panel there, which is the same panel under the same menu. Select the stage on the board. The eight footprints, every track that runs between them, any via those tracks use, and the four silkscreen labels at the motor terminals. Then right-click DRV8833-Stage in the panel and choose “Update Design Block from Selection”.

Do not reach for “Save Selection as Design Block…” on this side. That command makes a new block, and what you would end up with is two entries in the library, one holding a circuit with no copper and one holding copper with no circuit. The layout has to go into the block that already holds the schematic, and “Update Design Block from Selection” is the command that puts it there.

Let the schematic editor pick the parts for you. This is my preferred way of making sure I have selected every part that belongs to the block, and only those. Dragging a box around eight footprints on a busy board is difficult. There is a better way, that makes use of KiCad’s cross-probing feature1. Select the eight symbols on the schematic, and their footprints come up selected on the board beside it. The parts are then chosen for you, correctly, with no box drawn at all. What is left is to add the tracks, the vias and the labels by hand, and those you can pick off one at a time (Figure 9.4).

Figure 9.4: Capturing the layout half, using cross-probing. Selecting the eight symbols on the schematic sheet (1) brings up the same eight footprints on the board (2), which is a reliable way to ensure you haven’t missed anything. The tracks, the vias and the silkscreen labels are added to that selection in the PCB editor. Then the block is right-clicked in the Design Blocks panel (3) and “Update Design Block from Selection” (4) writes the layout into the block that already holds the circuit. The description in the panel reads the same on this side as it did on the schematic side, because there is one block here and not two.

Be mindful of the following:

  1. Leave the board’s pours out, and include the chip’s own zone. There are two ground zones on this board, but only one should be included in the block. The two GND_1 pours are filled from a net that reaches every corner of the design, so they belong to the board but not the block. However, U1-GND-pads_1 belongs in the block. This is the small, high-priority zone sitting over U1’s exposed pad, giving it a solid connection instead of thermal spokes. A second chip needs its own solid connection over its own exposed pad. If you leave this zone out, you will have to draw a new 6.1 by 4.0 mm rectangle by hand, in the same place.
  2. The thermal vias come automatically. The twelve vias under U1’s exposed pad are part of the footprint you drew in Chapter 2, rather than board objects sitting on top of it, so they travel with the footprint and there is nothing to select.
  3. Take the U1_breakout rule area with you. The second chip needs the same region for the same reason the first one does, so include it in the selection.

That third item comes across, but not intact. Open the block’s .kicad_pcb after the update and U1_breakout is in there as a rule area with its keepout settings unchanged, so the shape you drew in Chapter 8 arrives with the second stage and does not need to be redrawn. The name is the part that does not survive. KiCad makes area names unique as it pastes them, so the second stage’s copy arrives as U1_breakout_1, and the condition A.intersectsArea('U1_breakout') in your .kicad_dru then covers one chip out of the two. The section on laying out two thermal fields shows what that costs on the board, and renames both areas to fix it.

Figure 9.5: What the selection holds. Eight footprints, the tracks and vias between them, the hatched U1_breakout rule area around the chip, and the four silkscreen labels at the motor terminals. The twelve thermal vias under the exposed pad are not selected because they are already part of U1.

Nothing reaches the library until you click “Update Design Block from Selection”, so take as many passes over the board as you need to get the selection right. Holding “Shift” while you click or drag adds to the selection instead of starting a new one, so the cross-probed footprints, then the tracks, the vias, the four silkscreen labels and the two areas all accumulate into one selection that stays live while you work. Click the command once, when that selection is complete. It replaces the block’s layout half with whatever is selected at the moment you click, so a run made part-way through writes nothing that the final one would not write anyway, and on the version I built this board with, running it more than once also triggered a bug. The aside below explains it.

A bug I found here, reported, and saw fixed. “Update Design Block from Selection” writes the layout into the library, and it also groups whatever is selected, linking that new group to the block. On 10.0.5, running it again on a selection that already contained the first run’s group wrapped that group inside a new one instead of extending it. Repeat it and the groups nest, each carrying the same name; the board draws an outline for each, and the library stores the whole nest inside the block, so every stage you later stamp from it inherits the nest. Nothing anywhere reports this, because none of it is electrically wrong.

Selecting the linked group on its own always worked correctly. Only a selection larger than the group triggered the fault, which is exactly what you get if you run the command, add a few more objects and run it again. I filed it against 10.0.5 as issue #25368, it was confirmed the same day, and the fix went into a nightly build a few days later, so it will have reached a stable release by the time you read this or will do soon. Check the issue to see where it stands in your version. Chapter 10, Intermediate Recipes, uses this one as the worked example in its section on reporting a KiCad bug, if you would like to see what went into the report.

How to undo a nest made on an older version. A nest does not unmake itself when you upgrade, and nothing is at risk in the fixing. Select the outermost group, choose “Group”, then “Ungroup”, and repeat until nothing on the stage is grouped any more; the objects themselves are untouched throughout, because a group holds references rather than copies. Then select the whole stage once and update the block a single time. Ungroup all the way down before you update, because updating from a selection that still contains a group writes that group into the library along with everything else.

The preview at the foot of the panel shows the captured layout rather than the circuit, because the panel previews whichever half belongs to the editor you are standing in. As you know, a design block’s two halves are saved separately. But KiCad has no way to compare them. If you capture the schematic, change something on the board, and then capture the layout, you will have a block whose circuit and whose copper describe two different stages. It will place without complaint, but KiCad will not tell you that there is a discrepancy until you discover it on your own.

So capture both halves in one sitting, from a board whose design rules check is clean and whose schematic you have not modified since. That is an example of the kind of discipline you need to develop. It is also why you should capture from a finished board instead of one under development.

Both halves are now grouped, and linked. Saving the schematic selection made the eight symbols a linked group on the sheet. “Update Design Block from Selection” has just done the same on the board, putting the eight footprints, the tracks between them, their vias, the four silkscreen labels, the U1-GND-pads_1 zone and the U1_breakout rule area into a group of their own, with a “Library link” field reading klp5e-ch09-blocks:DRV8833-Stage. There is nothing left to group by hand.

The link will keep the library current. If you improve the stage later, on the sheet or on the board, right-click its group and choose “Save to Linked Design Block”. That writes the improved stage into the library over the version you have just saved. Without the link, every improvement would mean another trip through “Update Design Block from Selection”, with the selection drawn correctly again each time.

One caveat about which group you save from, which matters later in this chapter. This works from stage one’s board group only until the section on placing the second stage tidies that group up. After that, the copy is the instance to save from, because its group is the one holding both the footprints and the copper.

The block is in the library and it holds both halves. Let’s use it.

Placing the second stage

Placing the block is quick. Then, we’ll take a bit of time to turn that block into the second stage.

Place it on the sheet. Find DRV8833-Stage in the Design Blocks panel, and set four checkboxes below the preview before you place anything. They are given here in the order the panel prints them (Figure 9.7).

  • “Place repeated copies”, clear. If ticked, KiCad will give you another copy as soon as you have put the last one down, and goes on doing it until you press “Esc”. That is the right setting for a board that wants multiples of a block. In this example, we only want one copy, aside from the one already on the sheet.
  • “Place as group”, ticked. A saved layout can only be applied to a design block that is a group on the schematic and a group on the board. Therefore, an ungrouped copy would give you the circuit and leave the copper behind.
  • “Place as sheet”, clear, so that the copy you are about to make goes straight onto the sheet you are looking at. There is a real alternative behind this one, and the two paragraphs after the next figure explain what it does.
  • “Keep annotations”, clear. If you tick this box, the copy arrives carrying U1, C2, C3, C4, R2, R3, J2 and J3, every one of which already belongs to the first stage. Clearing it strips those designators instead, and on the version I built this board with the eight parts arrive carrying question marks: U?, three C?, two R? and two J?. You give them numbers of their own in a few minutes, once both groups are named.

Then double-click the block, or right-click it and choose “Place Design Block”, and click once on a clear part of the sheet.

The four placement checkboxes (2) are set as the text describes, with only “Place as group” ticked. The arrow (3) marks where this copy went, which was a clear patch of sheet below the first stage. In this step, we simply want to copy the block in a relatively empty space in the sheet.

What is “Place as sheet”? If you tick this option, the block copy will go into a hierarchical sheet of its own instead of onto the current one. This can be useful under the right conditions, but you need to understand the consequences. If you give the second stage a sheet of its own, you will need to make necessary adjustments to the net labels and utilise hierarchical sheet labels to allow connections between the nets across the two sheets.

Why not do that here? Because it increases the workload without any particular benefits. A new sheet allows us to retain the existing block labels, but it adds work to the four that must be shared, as each of those then needs a hierarchical label inside the sheet and a matching sheet pin outside it. On this stage the twelve are simple text edits and the four would be new drawing, so placing the copy inline costs less. If you had a different project with the proportions in reverse, on a board that repeats one block three, four, or more times, then the sheet is the better option. Chapter 10’s multi-channel recipe works that case through.

Before doing anything else, let’s tidy up. The Chapter 8 sheet was arranged for one stage, so there was a lot of free space in the sheet. With the two stages, we need to be more efficient with how we allocate the space in a single sheet.

I decided to arrange the two stages side by side across the top of the sheet, and run everything that exists once along the bottom. The supply input and its protection, the bulk capacitor, the indicator, the control header, the fault pull-up and the four mounting holes. No connection changes.

Both stages are groups now, but both are called DRV8833-Stage. That is the block’s name rather than the instance’s, and with two instances on the sheet using the same name is problematic. Select each group in turn, press “E” or right-click and choose “Properties…”, and set the Name field: DRV8833-Stage-1 for the original and DRV8833-Stage-2 for the copy.

The “Library link” field sits directly below the name in the same dialogue, and it is not the field you are editing. Leave it alone and both groups go on pointing at klp5e-ch09-blocks:DRV8833-Stage, so “Apply Design Block Layout” and everything else that depends on that link keeps working. Renaming a group renames the instance, not the block it came from.

The names give you a way to verify which stage you are working on. The Groups tab of the Search panel then lists the two by name with their positions, which is a quick way to confirm you have two groups instead of one group and a loose collection of parts.

That dialogue has just shown you two of the three ways this chapter names a set of parts. They are easy to confuse, so here they are separated once:

  1. The library link says which block a stage came from. It is what makes “Apply Design Block Layout” and “Save to Linked Design Block” work, and it reads the same on both stages, because both stages came from the same block.
  2. The group name tells the two instances apart, for you and for the Search panel. No design rule reads it.
  3. A component class is how you address a stage in a design rule or a variant. There is not one on this board yet. The section on laying out two thermal fields gives stage two’s eight parts a class of their own, and the section on one project, two boards is where it pays for itself.

You could select the eight and let the Annotate tool fill them in, but it numbers by position, and this board’s numbering is not positional, so you would be correcting its choices afterwards. Chapter 8 made the same point when it asked you to rename a handful of designators by hand, J1 to J4 among them. Set them manually in the Symbol Fields Table or on the sheet, whichever you find quicker; I did mine by hand so that they matched the table exactly.

KiCad’s manual says it should have numbered them, but it did not. The manual’s description of “Keep annotations” says that clearing the box makes KiCad reannotate the block as it inserts it, following your annotation settings. Two things stop that here.

The first is that automatic annotation has to be switched on for anything to be assigned at all. That is the “Automatically annotate symbols” setting in Preferences, then “Schematic Editor”, then “Editing Options”, with a matching toggle button on the left toolbar. With it off, the eight arrive as question marks which you will then have to edit manually.

The second applies even with it on. The reannotation does not happen when the block is placed as a group, which is how this chapter places it. Place the same block ungrouped and the numbers appear. That is issue #22620, “Symbols are not automatically annotated when placing design block as group”, reported against a 9.99 development build and still open when I checked it on 2 September 2026. Open that link and see where it stands in your version, because if it has been fixed by the time you read this, and you have automatic annotation switched on, your eight parts will arrive numbered rather than blank.

The step above works either way. KiCad numbers by position, but this board’s numbering is not positional, so whether you are typing eight designators into blank fields or correcting eight that KiCad chose, the table is what you are matching them against.

The second stage is beside the first, and the parts that exist once have moved to the bottom of the sheet: J1 and Q1 with the bulk capacitor and the indicator, the header J4 with its nFAULT pull-up R1, and the four mounting holes. Each stage carries its group outline with its own name, and the Groups tab of the Search panel lists the two of them. Also look at stage two’s labels: AOUT1, AIN1, AISEN and the rest, reading word for word the same as stage one’s. Twelve labels in stage two name a net that already belongs to the other chip.

A design block reproduces the labels it captured. Placing the block copied all sixteen of stage one’s net labels onto the sheet, names and all. KiCad merges any two labels that share a name into one net, so twelve of those sixteen now tie a pin on U2 to the matching pin on U1, and your second stage is wired in parallel with the first.

AOUT1 is the simplest case. It sits on U1 pin 2 in stage one and on U2 pin 2 in the copy, so one net named AOUT1 joins the two chips’ half-bridge outputs directly together. The other three bridge outputs are shorted in the same way, both sense returns are shorted, and all four direction inputs are ganged, so a command meant for motor A drives motor C as well.

The last two duplicates are VINT and VCP, which read like supply rails but are not. VINT is the output of the chip’s own internal regulator, brought to a pin so that you can place a 2.2 µF capacitor on it, and VCP is its charge pump2, brought out for the same kind of reason. Each belongs to one chip. Sharing VINT puts two regulator outputs in parallel and sharing VCP puts two charge pumps in parallel, and the datasheet allows neither.

Run the electrical rules check on this sheet and it reports five errors. Twelve labels are wrong. Here is a summary of what it caught and missed:

Duplicated label Pin type ERC
AOUT1, AOUT2, BOUT1, BOUT2 output error: two outputs on one net
VINT power output error: two power outputs on one net
AIN1, AIN2, BIN1, BIN2 input silent
AISEN, BISEN, VCP passive silent

Two outputs on one net is an illegal combination, so the four bridge outputs are reported. Two inputs on one net is legal, because that is how a single clock signal reaches two chips, so the four direction inputs are not reported. Two passive pins raise no conflict, so AISEN, BISEN and VCP are silent as well. VINT and VCP differ in their type: Chapter 2 made pin 14 a power output and pin 11 passive, and that alone makes the duplicated VINT an error and the duplicated VCP silent. Nothing in the rules check can tell that AIN1 on this board is meant to command one motor and not two.

Rename the four bridge outputs and VINT, and the five errors clear. The sheet then passes with seven of the twelve duplicated labels untouched. Both sense lines are still shorted, all four direction inputs are still ganged, and each of those seven nets still ties the two chips together. Chapters 7 and 8 made the same point. Remember that a clean ERC is not proof that the schematic is correct.

Before you rename anything, answer this question: which of stage two’s names identify a net that both stages share? The answer is not obvious. It is these four:

  • GND. This board should have only one ground return. Both chips’ ground pins and exposed pads land on it. This one is not a label but the ground symbol from the power library.
  • VM. One supply should feed both stages, which is the architecture the section on the four-motor board set out. The label is correct, but the copper behind it is not, and the next section will address this.
  • nSLEEP. Both chips enable together, so that the controller only needs one pin for this signal. If you want the two stages to be able to sleep independently, rename the copy nSLEEP2 along with everything else and take one more pin on the header.
  • nFAULT. Both chips report on one line. This is a property of the part rather than a choice. The pin is open-drain, so it can pull the line down only. If you tie both pins to one node under a single pull-up, the line will mean that “at least one of these chips has a fault”. This requires no extra components, and it guarantees that the two outputs cannot fight each other.

Sharing power

The second stage duplicated the driver half of the board. The power half was not duplicated. One supply input, one bulk capacitor, one reverse-polarity MOSFET and one power indicator feed both stages, and they now carry twice the current they did in Chapter 8.

This section does two things:

  1. It explains why some parts are shared and others are repeated.
  2. It checks the shared parts, and the board setup that describes them, against 6 A instead of 3 A.

You might be wondering why the bulk capacitor is shared while the ceramics are not. When a bridge switches3, the current it draws changes within nanoseconds. That demand has to be met from very close by, because every millimetre of track between the chip and a more distant source carries inductance which is what resists a fast change in current. The loop that matters runs out of the chip’s VM pin, through the capacitor, and back to the chip’s ground. What sets the inductance of that loop is its physical size.

The requirement on C2 is therefore geometric instead of electrical. It has to sit at U1’s pin, which is where Chapter 8 measured it at 2.90 mm. A second chip 25 mm away sits at the far end of a much larger loop, which is too far. Every chip needs one of its own, which is why the section on capturing the driver stage as a design block put all three ceramics inside the block.

C1 handles a slower change. As a motor starts, stalls or reverses, the average current drawn from the supply changes over milliseconds, and the supply itself sits at the far end of the wire you have screwed into J1. The resistance and the inductance of that wire are common to both stages, because both stages are downstream of the same terminal block, and one capacitor at that point maintains the rail voltage for both of them.

Putting a second electrolytic beside the second chip would place two capacitors in parallel across one node. Two capacitors in parallel behave as one larger capacitor. This arrangement would not shorten the loop, since the fast loop is already covered by the ceramics and the slow one is shared. The only thing the second capacitor would change is how much capacitance and how much ripple-current rating sit at that one point. That is a question of size, not of arrangement, so C1 stays a single capacitor and the doubled current is met by what that one capacitor is rated for, which is what the list below works through.

So, as you can see, the test to help with making these decisions is which loop the part sits in. A part in a loop that belongs to one chip has to be repeated. A part in a loop that both chips share does not.

Focus on the three shared parts. The doubled current reaches these parts differently, so take them one at a time.

  • J1, the supply input. The Phoenix MKDS 1,5/2-5,08 is rated at 17.5 A, so 6 A is well within it. The wire you screw into it is the more likely limit.
  • Q1, the reverse-polarity MOSFET. The power a resistance dissipates rises with the square of the current, so doubling the current has quadrupled the heat in this part. It is still nowhere near what the part is built for: the Vishay datasheet rates the SUD45P03-09 at 45 A continuous, and at 32 A when the gate has only 4.5 V to work with, so 6 A leaves a fivefold margin even in the worse of those two cases. Heat is the only question left, and the arithmetic is below the list.
  • C1, the bulk capacitor. This is the only one of the three where the doubling approaches a published limit. The EEU-FR1E101 is rated for 455 mA of ripple current4, and ripple current is what doubles when twice as much switching load is put on one capacitor. The DRV8833 datasheet does not settle this value for you. Its Section 9.1 lists what feeds into it, which is the peak current, the inductance of your supply leads, the ripple you will accept and whether the motor is braked, and then says that system-level testing is what decides. So measure this one on a real board instead of calculating it, and fit the 105 °C low-ESR part.

Q1 is the one to work through, because the answer depends on something outside the part. This circuit drives the gate from the supply itself, so your supply voltage decides which row of the SUD45P03-09 datasheet applies, and a lower supply gives a higher on-resistance. The DRV8833’s Recommended Operating Conditions table caps VM at 10.8 V, and its Absolute Maximum Ratings table stops at 11.8 V, so 10.8 V is the most this board may be run at and it is the first row below. The second row is a 5 V supply, which is the other common case.

Supply Datasheet gate-drive row On-resistance Dissipation at 3 A Dissipation at 6 A

10.8 V 10 V 0.0087 Ω 0.078 W 0.313 W

At 10.8 V, 0.313 W in a package rated at 60 °C/W corresponds to a rise of about 19 °C, which is comfortable. When you scale up this board, remember that as the current doubles, the heat quadruples.

Q1 would be just as comfortable at 12 V, or at 24 V, and the DRV8833 would not. Q1 drops about 50 mV at 6 A, so whatever you screw into J1 is what arrives at both chips’ VM pins, and 12 V there is past the absolute maximum the datasheet allows. A protection device’s rating is not the board’s rating, and on this board the chips are what set the ceiling.

As we did in Chapter 8, here too we will size the class for its worst case. The Power class holds *VM*, *GND and *OUT*, and its worst case is VM. The eight bridge outputs will now default to 3.80 mm of copper when each of them carries at most 1.5 A and needs 0.53 mm.

Trace the supply from the terminal block. VM_IN arrives at J1, passes through Q1 and becomes VM at the bulk capacitor. Up to that point all the current for both chips (up to 6 A) flows in one piece of copper. Past the point where the rail divides, one branch runs to U1 and the other to U2, and each of those branches feeds one chip’s two bridges, which is 3 A. A net class cannot describe that, because a class gives one width to a whole net and VM is a single net running from the terminal block to both chips. So the trunk is drawn at 3.80 mm and the branches are necked down to 1.50 mm past the split.

That is why 1.50 mm stays on the “Pre-defined Sizes” list instead of being replaced by 3.80 mm. This list now reads 0.25, 0.30, 0.80, 1.50 and 3.80 mm. All these widths have a use on the board.

(rule "Power minimum width"

The 2.20 mm ceiling is the main problem in this rule, because the new board requires a minimum of 3.80 mm for the power rail. The other two numbers give no trouble. The control header

The board still carries Chapter 8’s connector, an eight-pin J4 sized for a single DRV8833. Four motors need more signals than that part can bring out, and U2’s four direction inputs, CIN1, CIN2, DIN1 and DIN2, are still nets that have to be routed to the header’s pins.

This section replaces that connector, in four steps:

  1. Work out how many signals the four-motor board has to bring out, which settles the pin count.
  2. Choose a part that carries them, and decide which signal sits on which pin.
  3. Swap J4 on the schematic without losing the labels and the ground symbol already drawn around it, then redraw the wiring.
  4. Prove the result with the electrical rules check and the netlist.

The fourth step finishes the schematic. After that, we’ll do the routing.

Count the pins before you choose the part. Chapter 8’s J4 brings eight signals to the edge of the board. Those signals are VLOGIC, GND, then nSLEEP and nFAULT, then the four direction inputs AIN1, AIN2, BIN1 and BIN2. The four motors need an additional eight direction inputs. The nSLEEP and nFAULT are shared. Therefore, the new header must provide twelve pins.

Twelve pins in a single row measure 31.48 mm, which is too long for the board. The same twelve in two rows of six measure 16.24 mm, which is shorter than the eight-pin part Chapter 8 fitted. So the header gains four pins and takes up less of the board edge than it did.

The length of the header is not the only reason for this choice. A double-row 2.54 mm header is what a keyed ribbon socket plugs into, which provides a convenient and tidy way to connect the board to a controller. So J4 becomes Conn_02x06_Odd_Even on the footprint PinHeader_2x06_P2.54mm_Vertical. The library that contains this footprint is Connector_Generic.

KiCad’s three 2x6 symbols hold the same twelve pins in the same two columns and differ only in the numbering. Conn_02x06_Odd_Even numbers them the way a ribbon header is numbered, with pins 1 and 2 side by side on the first row, 3 and 4 on the second, and so on down to 11 and 12 on the sixth. Conn_02x06_Counter_Clockwise and Conn_02x06_Top_Bottom number the same pads differently, so either would put pin 1 where a ribbon cable does not expect to find it. Take the odd/even symbol, or fit any other twelve-pin connector you prefer and carry the assignments below across to it.

The symbol has settled the geometry, which is twelve pins arriving as six numbered pairs. Which signal goes on which pair is still open, and it is a decision you make. I arranged the pin to signal pairs as per the table below.

nFAULT lands on a net that the two chips already drive. This is correct because of the kind of pin they drive it with. The aside below says how it works, since this is a property of the DRV8833, not relating to KiCad.

Why two chips can share one fault line. nFAULT is an open-drain output. This means that behind the pin sits a single transistor to the chip’s internal ground so that the pin can pull its line low or let go of it, but never drive it high. R1, the 10 kΩ resistor up to VLOGIC, is what puts the line high once both chips have let go. Two such pins on one node therefore cannot contradict each other, and the line ends up reporting “at least one of these chips has a fault”. The left half of Figure 9.21 depicts this arrangement.

The right half shows the same wiring applied to the bridge outputs, and those pins behave differently, because they are push-pull rather than open-drain. A push-pull output has two transistors behind the pin instead of one, an upper transistor to the supply and a lower one to ground, which the chip switches on one at a time and never together. The upper one “pushes” the line up to the supply and the lower one “pulls” it down to ground, which is where the name comes from, and it is how a bridge output swings a motor terminal either way. The consequence that matters here is that such a pin can drive its line high as well as low, where the open-drain pin above could only pull it down. Tie two of them to one node and one chip can drive the line high while the other drives it low, with nothing but the two transistors’ own resistance limiting the current that then runs from VM to ground straight through the pair. That is what the duplicated AOUT1, AOUT2, BOUT1 and BOUT2 labels created in the section on placing the second stage, and it is why renaming them was not optional.

The weakness of this arrangement is that the line reports a fault without indicating which chip raised it. On a four-wheeled robot that is usually enough information to stop the robot. But if your application requires more granularity so that it can detect which chip has raised the fault, then separate the two lines and use a second controller pin. R1 stays a single 10 kΩ part either way, and that is why the section on capturing the driver stage as a design block kept it outside the block.

On the right, the same wiring on two push-pull outputs. One chip’s high-side device is on, the other’s low-side is on, and the current runs straight from VM to ground through the two of them. One project, two boards

The board as it stands describes one product, which is drawn for four motors. In this section I’ll show you how to extract the second product, without changing anything on the board itself. The mechanism is design variants, which Chapter 5, Bill of Materials, In Depth, taught in its section on design variants and DNP. With design variants, one base design stays the single definitive version, with named sets of overrides laid on top of it. Chapter 5’s capstone used a field override, because its sense resistors could not be left off. This board uses the other kind, a fitting override, which we will apply to a stage.

Let’s consider why do-not-populate is the right override here. Stage two’s eight parts leave as a unit, and every connection they carried leaves with them. Stage one keeps its own complete current paths, and the shared nets need no change. VM and GND feed one chip exactly as they fed two. nSLEEP wakes the board with one chip listening instead of two, and nFAULT still reports, because one open-drain pin under one pull-up is still a working fault line. The four direction inputs for motors C and D end on pads with nothing fitted to them, and a net that reaches an empty pad is simply a net that stops there.

Those eight parts are the ones named by the Stage-2 class, which the section on laying out two thermal fields created.

Create a new design variant. Open the variant selector in the schematic editor’s toolbar, choose “Add New Design Variant”, and name it two-motor. Then open the Symbol Fields Table and select two-motor in the variant list down its left side, so that what follows lands in the variant and not in the base design. Check that selection before you tick anything.

Now, use the component class. In the field list down the left side, tick “Group By” against Component Class and against nothing else. The sheet collapses into two rows, and the lower one is stage two: eight symbols, class Stage-2. Design variants and component classes are at their most useful together, and this is what they give us here: two products off the same panel. You order one fabrication, and the two-motor build comes out of it as the four-motor build minus eight parts at assembly.

What they do not give you is a different bare board. The unpopulated stage is still in the fabrication outputs. The pads, the holes and the COUT and DOUT silkscreen labels remain, and so does the routed copper that fed the second chip, on a board where nothing will be soldered to it. That leftover copper is also an upgrade path, because the two-motor board keeps a place for the second stage, and fitting it later is a quick assembly job.

The risk sits in the schematic rather than on the board. A wrong tick in the fields table does not change the copper, so the flags go out with the files you send for fabrication and assembly. In this case, the outcome is an incorrect bill of materials.

Always read the bill before you send it to the manufacture, and don’t trust it blindly. Put ${VARIANT} into the export path, as in bom_${VARIANT}.csv, and export once per variant from the dialogue you have just been working in. That gives each build its own bill, and reading the two side by side is the fastest check that the variant marks what you meant and nothing else.

Start with the file name, which is a check in itself. ${VARIANT} resolves to an empty string under < Default >, so a file that lands as bom_.csv tells you the do-not-populate flags went into the base design rather than into two-motor. Nothing in the fields table will have said a word about it.

That happens whenever a direction input changes, and repeatedly while the chip chops the current to hold it at the limit. That alternating current passes through the capacitor’s own internal resistance, its equivalent series resistance or ESR, and dissipates heat inside the can as I²R. A maximum ripple current is therefore a thermal rating: how much of this current the part can carry without heating itself past what it is rated for. Electrolytic capacitors are the most sensitive to it, which is why the datasheet states the number, and why it is the number the doubling approaches.↩︎

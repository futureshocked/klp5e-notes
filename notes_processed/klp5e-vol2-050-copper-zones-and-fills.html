Copper Zones and Fills

In the last chapter you set up the board and assigned its nets to net classes, so the power nets are routed with wide tracks and the logic nets with narrow ones. The next step is to fill the empty space between the tracks with copper. This copper has two jobs on a board like this one. It is the ground return path1 that carries the motor current back to its source, and it provides a common voltage reference for the whole circuit. KiCad calls an area of copper like this a ‘filled zone’. In this chapter you will learn how to create filled zones, how to stop copper from filling the areas where you do not want it, and how to manage a board that contains several zones.

Filled Zones

A filled zone is a region of copper, tied to a net, that KiCad fills for you and refills each time the board changes. You can create a filled zone by drawing an outline, telling KiCad which net the copper belongs to and which layer it sits on, and then the fill spreads into all the space inside that outline, automatically leaving the right clearance around every track, pad, and via that is not on its net. The most common use by far is a ground pour, and it is the one this board needs. This copper fill is a zone on the “GND” net that covers the board, so that every ground connection has a short, wide path back to the ground net.

A zone is not a static drawing. KiCad refills it automatically when you run the design rules check, when you generate the fabrication outputs, and whenever you manually trigger a refill (for example using the “B” hotkey), so it always reflects the current state of the surrounding tracks. That means you can pour early and keep routing; the copper refills around each new track as you place it.

Creating a Zone

Four steps make a zone, and the first three happen before you have drawn anything more than a single corner (Figure 4.2).

  1. Pick the “Add Filled Zone” tool from the right toolbar (❶).
  2. Click in the canvas at the point where you want the outline to start (❷). That click is what opens the “Copper Zone Properties” dialogue, because KiCad needs to know two things before it will let you draw: which layer or layers the zone lives on, and which net its copper belongs to.
  3. Fill in the properties (❸). At a minimum, set the net and tick the layer. The next section takes the rest of this dialogue apart field by field.
  4. Click “OK” (❹), and carry on placing corners. Click the first corner again, or double-click, to close the outline.

Figure 4.2: Starting a zone. The “Add Filled Zone” button on the right toolbar (❶) is armed, the first corner has been clicked at the crosshair (❷), and that click has brought up “Copper Zone Properties” (❸) with the “OK” button at the foot of it (❹). This is not another ground pour. The zone being built here is the small “VM_Zone” on the motor-supply net, which is why “Net name” reads /VM and why the corner smoothing is set to “Fillet” with a 2 mm radius rather than the “None” the ground pours use.

Draw the rest of the outline and the zone appears, filled, with its settings visible in the Properties panel (Figure 4.3). The outline in this figure has square corners, and that is a choice. The line-mode button on the left toolbar (❷) cycles between free angle, 90 degree, and 45 degree modes, and it governs the placement of tracks, zone outlines, graphics, and dimensions alike. You can use “Shift+Space” to toggle it without leaving the canvas.

Figure 4.3: The finished outline (❶), drawn in 90 degree line mode (❷) so that every corner is square. The Properties panel (❸) now describes a real object: net /VM, name “VM_Zone”, and, importantly, priority 1. The ground pour underneath it is priority 0, and that difference is what determines which of the two fills the copper where they overlap. At the bottom, a third row has appeared in the Search panel’s “Zones” tab (❹), so the board now carries “GND_bottom”, “GND_top”, and “VM_Zone”.

The result is two zones sharing the top copper layer (Figure 4.4). This is worth looking at closely, because it is the first time on this board that two pours cover the same space. The higher-priority zone fills first and takes the overlap; the lower-priority one fills around whatever is left. Nothing is lost and nothing shorts, because each zone still keeps its clearance from the other’s copper.

Figure 4.4: Two zones on F.Cu. The ground pour (❶) covers the board, and the motor-supply pour (❷), shown with its outline hatching, sits inside it over the section of the board where the VM tracks run. Because “VM_Zone” has the higher priority, the ground pour does not fill that rectangle.

Zone Properties

The dialogue you filled in at step 3 is short, but almost every field in it changes the copper you get. Figure 4.5 shows it for the board’s top ground pour, with six things marked.

Figure 4.5: “Copper Zone Properties” for the top ground pour, with the zone it describes visible behind it (❶). The settings are grouped as layers (❷), the optional name (❸), the net (❹), the clearance and pad-connection block (❺), and the two fill-behaviour dropdowns at the foot (❻).

Layers (❷). Here only “F.Cu” is ticked, but the list allows any combination. One zone outline can fill on several copper layers at once. The copper on each layer is calculated independently, so a via on one layer does not punch a hole in another, but all of them share the single net you set below. That is a genuine convenience on a multi-layer board: draw the outline once, tick four layers, and get four coordinated pours.

Zone name (❸). Optional, and you should fill it in every time. There are two main reasons for this recommendation. The first is that a named zone is easily identifiable in the Zone Manager’s list. The second is that the name is how the rest of KiCad refers to the zone. A custom design rule can refer to a zone by name, so naming a zone now is what makes it addressable later. This board’s three pours are called “GND_top”, “GND_bottom”, and “VM_Zone”.

Net name (❹). The net the copper belongs to, “GND” here. A zone with no net at all is legal. Such a zone keeps its clearance from the copper on every net, and it connects to no net. That is occasionally what you want for a shield or a mechanical pad.

Clearances and pad connections (❺). These five fields sit together on the “Clearances & Pad Connections” tab, and they read down the tab in the same order as the bullets below: “Clearance” first, then “Minimum width”, then the three that work together, “Pad connections”, “Thermal relief gap”, and “Thermal spoke width”. The first two are easy to skim past:

  • Clearance (0.2 mm), the top field, is the minimum gap the filled copper keeps from other copper objects. Where this value and the net class’s value differ, KiCad applies the larger one, so the 0.3 mm you gave the Power class in the last chapter still governs the power nets even though this field reads 0.2 mm. Setting a small number here does not undo a larger rule elsewhere.
  • Minimum width (0.2 mm), directly beneath it, is the narrowest neck of copper the fill is allowed to create. Anything thinner is dropped during the fill. This is what stops the fill from creating hairline slivers of copper between two tracks that pass close together. Slivers like these are hard to etch reliably and do no electrical work.
  • Pad connections, Thermal relief gap (0.5 mm) and Thermal spoke width (0.3 mm), the last three fields on the tab, control how the pour meets the pads on its own net. They matter enough to have the next section to themselves.

Corner smoothing and remove islands (❻). Corner smoothing chamfers or fillets the corners of the filled copper, or, set to “None” as it is here, lets the fill run right into the corner of the outline. Remember that KiCad does not smooth inside corners by default, because the smoothed copper would have to bulge outside the outline you drew. If you want it to, enable “Allow fillets outside zone outline” in the “Constraints” section of Board Setup.

“Remove islands” is the more interesting of the two. An ‘island’ is a patch of the fill that ended up electrically isolated from the rest of its own net. KiCad produced it when it filled the zone, and it is on the zone’s net, yet there is no copper path from it back to that net, because tracks, pads, or a keep-out separate it from the rest. An island is not an error in itself. It is simply copper that may or may not be doing anything for you, and the dropdown is where you say which.

  • Always, the setting this board uses, deletes every island. This is the appropriate default. An isolated patch of copper does no electrical work, and a large patch can act as an antenna, picking up noise and re-radiating it.
  • Never keeps them all. Choose this when the “island” is deliberate. For example, the island may be a copper area you intend to connect by hand, a pad added for mechanical strength, or a patch of copper acting as a heat spreader.
  • Below area limit deletes islands smaller than a threshold you set, which keeps the large, useful patches and removes the small ones. The threshold field appears next to the dropdown when you choose this mode, and the Properties panel in Figure 4.1 shows this board’s threshold, 10 mm², which is unused because the zone is set to “Always”.

One caveat from KiCad’s own rules is that islands are only ever removed from a zone that has at least one electrical connection. A zone that connects to nothing keeps all of its copper, whatever this setting says. The reason is that no part of such a zone reaches its net, so every part of it counts as an island. Removing them would delete the entire zone without warning you, and KiCad does not do that.

Pad Connections and Thermal Relief

The most important setting on a zone is how it connects to the pads on its own net. In the zone’s properties you choose a ‘pad connection’ mode. A ‘solid’ connection fills copper right up to the pad, so that the pad is completely surrounded by the zone copper. A ‘thermal relief’ connection instead joins the pad to the surrounding copper with a few narrow spokes, leaving a small gap of bare board around the rest of the pad. There are two more choices. ‘Reliefs for PTH’ uses thermal spokes on through-hole pads but solid connections on surface-mount ones, and ‘none’ leaves the pad unconnected to the zone entirely. Two values control the relief itself: the ‘thermal relief gap’, which is the width of the bare ring around the pad, and the ‘thermal spoke width’, which is the width of each connecting spoke.

This setting matters because of heat, and specifically the heat from the soldering iron rather than the heat from the chip. When a pad has a solid connection to a large copper pour, that pour conducts the heat away as soon as you apply the iron, and the joint never gets hot enough for the solder to flow cleanly. Thermal reliefs make a pad easier to solder because the soldering heat cannot dissipate into the large copper area so quickly through those few thin spokes. Of course, this choice has a consequence. A solid connection gives the chip the best cooling it can get, because the whole pour is available to carry heat away from the pad, and it gives you a pad that is hard to solder by hand. Thermal relief reverses both. The pad solders easily, but the only copper carrying heat out of it is the few narrow spokes, so the part runs warmer once it is working. Neither answer is wrong. The right one depends on whether this board will be reflowed in an oven or built by hand at your bench, and you now know which setting records that decision.

The difference is easier to see than to describe, so here is the same pad under both settings. Figure 4.6 is the solid connection.

Figure 4.6: Three pads of the header, magnified, with the ground pour set to “Solid”. Pad 6 is on “GND”, the pour’s own net, and the pour has filled right up to it: copper runs straight up to the ring with no gap and no spokes, so the pad and the plane are one continuous piece of metal. Do not read the dark rings around pads 5 and 4 as reliefs. Those two pads are on “VM” and “AOUT1”, other nets entirely, so what you are seeing there is ordinary clearance, and it looks the same whichever pad-connection mode you pick.

Change the one dropdown and the same pad looks like Figure 4.7 instead.

Figure 4.7: The same header pad with the setting changed to “Thermal reliefs”. The pour no longer touches the ring. It reaches it through four narrow spokes, marked by the yellow arrows, each one the 0.3 mm the “Thermal spoke width” field specifies, and the dark wedges between them are the 0.5 mm “Thermal relief gap”. Pad 5 below it has not changed at all, because it is on “VM” and was never connected to this pour in the first place. The three fields that produced the difference are boxed on the right.

This board takes the thermal reliefs, on all three pours, because of the header. J1 is a through-hole part, and pin 6 sits on the ground net. If that pin is soldered solid into a pour that covers the entire board, it becomes the hardest joint on this board to solder by hand. The pour carries the heat away faster than the iron can supply it, so you hold the tip on the pad for a long time, melt the plastic of the header body, and still get a dull, grey joint. I have melted more than one header body this way, which is why I check this setting before pouring a board that has through-hole ground pins on it. Four spokes fix it. At 0.3 mm each they put 1.2 mm of copper into the pad in total, far more than the current through a single header pin needs. Since a pad connection is a per-zone setting, that decision is recorded three times, once on each pour, and you can read it back on any of them in the Zone Manager at the end of this chapter.

However, the setting does have a cost. Fine-pitch surface-mount pads do not always have room for the full set of spokes, and when a pad falls short, the design rules check reports a ‘starved thermal’2. On this board that happens once, at U1 pad 13, which reaches the pour through a single spoke.

Expect to see this violation in the DRC fairly often. The constraint behind it is “Minimum thermal relief spoke count”, on the “Constraints” page of Board Setup, and the KiCad default is 2. It is on by default on every board you start, so a single-spoke pad becomes an error in the report. Two settings control what happens. The first is the constraint itself, which takes any value from 0 to 4, where 0 turns the constraint off. The second is its severity, on the “Violation Severity” page you met in the last chapter. This board accepts the defaults for both, which is why its report shows the error.

When you do get a thermal relief spoke count violation, what should you do about it? As usual, the answer is “it depends”. On this board the correct answer is to accept it. This is because the chip’s ground does not depend on that spoke. Pad 17, the exposed pad sitting immediately beside pad 13, is on the same net, and you gave it a solid zone connection back in Chapter 2, so the pour fills right up to it with unbroken copper and continues to the bottom pour through its twelve vias. Ground reaches the part whatever happens to one 0.3 mm strip of copper. That strip is not marginal either; 0.3 mm is comfortably within what any fabricator can etch reliably. The spoke-count constraint is a robustness guideline about how firmly a pad is anchored into a pour, not a manufacturing floor of the kind the other numbers on the Constraints page represent, and here the anchoring is done by the exposed pad beside it.

In my example, I record the decision rather than ignoring the violation. Right-click the violation in the design rules check dialogue and choose “Exclude with comment…”, then write down the reason you just worked out. The exclusion survives between runs, and it reappears, with your comment attached, whenever you tick the “Exclusions” box, so the next person to open this board reads your reasoning instead of finding nothing. Do not take the other option, which is setting the severity to “Ignore”, because that turns the check off for every pad on the board, including the one where a single spoke would be a genuine problem. The last chapter made this point in general terms on the “Violation Severity” page, and this is the specific case it was describing. An exclusion applies to the one violation you examined, and your comment records why you accepted it. Setting the severity to “Ignore” applies to every pad on the board, and nothing on the board records that you did it.

If you would rather not make the judgement at all, “Reliefs for PTH” is the mode intended for boards like this one, giving spokes to the through-hole pads that need them and solid connections to the surface-mount pads that do not. Set it on the three pours, and you can read both halves of that description off the two figures you have just studied. Pin 6 of J1 is a plated through-hole pad, so it keeps the four spokes of Figure 4.7 and stays easy to solder by hand. Every ground pad on the DRV8833 is surface-mount, so each one fills solid, the way pad 6 does in Figure 4.6. Pad 13’s starved thermal goes with them, because a solid connection has no spokes for the constraint to count. That is a perfectly good way to settle this board, and the only reason I did not take it is that I would rather the board carry a written record of the one pad I thought about.

There is a case where the answer goes the other way. When the starved pad is a net’s only connection to the pour, that one spoke is not redundant, and you fix the layout instead of recording an exclusion. Remember that there is not a single track on the “GND” net anywhere on this board, so every ground pad you have is connected by the pours and nothing else. A ground pad placed very close to the board edge, or with a wide power track on one side and a neighbouring pour on the other, can come out with one spoke and no other copper reaching it, and 0.3 mm of copper is then carrying that pad’s whole connection. Move the part in from the edge, reroute whatever is crowding the pad, raise the zone’s “Thermal spoke width”, or give that single pad a “Solid” connection in its own pad properties.

Via Stitching

On a two-layer board it is common to pour ground on both the top and the bottom, then connect the two pours with a regular pattern of vias so that they act as one continuous plane. This technique is called ‘via stitching’. Via stitching is worth the effort because two pours joined only at their pads are two separate conductors that happen to share a name, whereas two pours joined by a grid of vias behave like one thicker one, with a lower impedance return path and better heat spreading.

The tool to use is “Add Free-standing Vias” on the right toolbar. Click it, then click wherever you want a via, and repeat. Nine clicks in the corner of this board produce the grid in Figure 4.8.

Figure 4.8: Nine stitching vias in a three-by-three grid in the corner of the board, placed with the free-via tool (❶) and boxed at ❷. Each one was dropped onto bare ground pour, so each one picked up the “GND” net from the copper beneath it and now ties the top pour to the bottom one at that point. The board edge runs down the right of the image; the corner is a good place for stitching because no track or component needs that area.

What surprises people is where each via’s net comes from, because you never type it in. KiCad works it out from what is underneath:

  • Drop the via on a track or a pad, and it takes that net and stays synchronised with it from then on. On a track it also creates a joint at that point. KiCad records this by ticking “Automatically update via nets” in the via’s properties.
  • Drop it on bare zone copper, and it takes the zone’s net, but “Automatically update via nets” stays clear, so its net will not follow the zone’s afterwards.
  • Drop it where two or more zones overlap, and KiCad asks you which net you meant.
  • Drop it on nothing at all, and the via gets no net.

The second case is the one that applies to stitching. It is why every via in Figure 4.8 came out on “GND” although you never named the net.

You have already built one of these without calling it stitching. The via array under the DRV8833’s exposed pad, which you drew into the footprint back in Chapter 2, is the same idea reached from the other direction (Figure 4.9). Those vias carry the chip’s ground down to the bottom pour exactly as the corner grid does, and they carry its heat with it, which is the reason a thermal pad exists at all.

Figure 4.9: The other set of stitching vias on this board, the twelve under the DRV8833’s exposed pad. Each is labelled “17 / GND”, pad 17 being the exposed pad itself, because these are part of the custom footprint rather than free vias you placed on the board. Same technique, same effect on the copper, but they arrive with the component instead of being drawn one at a time.

Where should stitching vias go, and how many are enough? Place them where the return current flows. Put them close to the pads and vias that carry the most current back to the supply, and add one beside every via where a power net crosses to the other side, so that its return has a path across at the same point. Spread the rest through the areas of copper no track occupies, including the perimeter and the corners, which is what the grid in Figure 4.8 does. On a board like this one, carrying direct current and low-frequency switching, a via every 5 to 10 mm through the pour is plenty, and a denser mesh gains you nothing. Keep in mind that a via in the top pour is also a hole in the bottom pour, so a grid dense enough to cut the copper underneath into narrow strips raises the impedance of the bottom pour instead of lowering it. This is a different question from the one the recipes chapter works through, where the issue is how much current a single via barrel can carry; a stitching via bonds two pours rather than carrying a routed net.

Keep-Outs

Pouring copper is about filling space; sometimes you need the opposite, a region where certain things are forbidden. In KiCad that region is a ‘rule area’, which earlier versions of the program called a ‘keep-out’. A rule area is a shape you draw on the board that carries a small set of prohibitions. Inside it, you can forbid tracks, vias, pads, footprints, and zone fills, in any combination. Any forbidden object placed inside the area raises a design-rule error, so the rule you drew is enforced, not merely suggested.

You add one with the rule-area tool on the right toolbar, or with “Ctrl+Shift+K”. Click to place the first corner and KiCad opens the “Rule Area Properties” dialogue, where you pick the layers the area applies to and tick, on its keep-out list, which kinds of object to exclude. Then you finish drawing the outline, which can be any polygon you like, not just a rectangle. The last of those prohibitions is the most useful one on the board you are building. A rule area that forbids zone fills makes your ground pour stop at its outline, leaving an area of bare board inside it. That is how you keep copper away from a mounting hole, keep a pour clear of a connector’s moving parts, or create a deliberate gap between two sections of a board.

This board needs exactly one, around its 2.5 mm mounting hole (Figure 4.10). A screw through that hole will have a metal head sitting on the board surface, and if the pour reaches the hole, that head can short the copper around the hole.

Figure 4.10: Starting the rule area. The tool is armed on the right toolbar (❶) and the first corner has been clicked at the crosshair (❷), which opened “Rule Area Properties”. The area is named “Mounting hole” (❸), which is not only a label: a named area is quoted back to you in any design-rule violation it causes, and a custom rule can target it by that name. Both copper layers are ticked (❹), since a hole goes through the whole board, and “Edge.Cuts” further down the list is ticked too, which only draws the outline on that layer and has no bearing on the rules. The prohibitions themselves are on the “Keepouts” tab (❺): tracks, vias, and zone fills are excluded, while “Keep out pads” and “Keep out footprints” are both deliberately left clear. Those two are clear for the same reason. The mounting hole is itself a footprint with a non-plated hole, and it has to sit inside this very area, so excluding either would make the rule flag the object it was drawn to protect. It is a small thing, but it is the kind of small thing that produces a design-rule error you spend twenty minutes not understanding.

Draw the outline, and it appears immediately, though the copper does not move yet (Figure 4.11). Notice that the outline deliberately overhangs the board edge on two sides. That is fine, and it is the better way to draw it. KiCad clips every fill at “Edge.Cuts” regardless, so the part of the area hanging over the edge simply has nothing to act on, while the part inside is guaranteed to reach the true corner of the board with no sliver of copper remaining between the area and the edge. You can use this overhang technique with both copper pours and rule areas.

Figure 4.11: The completed rule area, its hatched outline drawn around the mounting hole and running off the board on two sides. The ground pour is still filled right up to the hole, because drawing a rule area does not by itself move any copper.

Press “B” to refill the zones and the rule takes effect (Figure 4.12). The fill now stops at the area’s outline, and the hole is left in bare board.

Figure 4.12: The same view after a refill. The pour now stops at the edge of the rule area, and the mounting hole is surrounded by bare board, with the fill nowhere near it.

The 3D viewer is the quickest way to confirm the whole chapter’s work at once (Figure 4.13). In the canvas, a dozen other layers sit on top of the copper. A rendering strips them away and shows you the copper on its own.

Figure 4.13: Both faces of the finished board. On the left, the top: the ground pour (❶) covering everything the tracks have not taken, the motor-supply pour (❷) as a slightly different shade above the chip, and the keep-out (❸) as bare board in the corner. On the right, the bottom: the second ground pour (❹) and the same keep-out (❺), which appears in the opposite corner only because you are looking at the board from the other side. The stitching vias show as a small grid of holes in both views, which is the visual proof that the two pours are joined.

Rule areas do more than keep things out. Because a rule area is a named region, you can attach a custom design rule that applies only inside it, giving you fine control over one patch of the board without touching the rest. Rule areas are also how KiCad marks the repeated sections of a multi-channel layout. When a board carries several identical copies of a circuit, you place and route one of them and let KiCad copy that work onto the others, and it fences each copy inside its own placement rule area so that it knows where one channel ends and the next begins. You will meet that twice more in this volume: the warm-up project in Chapter 7 repeats the same 8×8 matrix module four times, and Chapter 9’s four-motor extension repeats a motor stage, while the multi-channel recipe in Chapter 10 walks through the whole procedure. For now, treat the rule area as your tool for keeping copper out of a region, and remember that it is there the day a board needs a rule that applies to one patch of the board and nowhere else.

The Zone Manager (K9/K10)

One zone is easy to manage from the canvas. A real board quickly has several. A ground pour on the top, another on the bottom, and perhaps a small power pour feeding the motor supply. Finding each one separately to change a setting is slow work. KiCad 9 introduced the ‘Zone Manager’ to solve this problem, and it is a useful addition. Open it from “Tools” and “Zone Manager”, and you get a single dialogue listing every zone on the board, each with its name, its net, and the layers it occupies, alongside a live preview and the full settings for whichever zone you select (Figure 4.14). Instead of searching for each copper zone and editing it on its own, you review and adjust all of them in one place.

Figure 4.14: The Zone Manager with this board’s three pours in it. On the left (❶), a search box and a layer filter above the list of zones that match: “VM_Zone” on /VM, “GND_top” on GND, and “GND_bottom” on GND, each showing its layer. On the right (❷), the full settings for whichever zone is selected, “GND_top” here, above a live preview of the F.Cu fill. Everything in that pane is editable except the one thing that is not a setting at all, the outline itself, which stays a canvas job. At the foot (❸), a “Refill zones” checkbox and an “Update Displayed Zones” button, which is how you see the effect of a change before committing to it.

The Zone Manager is also where you set zone priority, which is an important change from earlier versions of KiCad. When two zones overlap on the same layer, priority determines which one fills first and therefore takes the overlapping area. In KiCad 10 that priority is the order of the zones in the manager’s list, where higher in the list means higher priority. You can still set an explicit priority number for each zone in the Properties panel, which is the value Figure 4.3 reported as priority 1, but the list is where you work with it. Look at the list in Figure 4.14, and you can read this board’s answer straight off it. “VM_Zone” sits at the top, above both ground pours, which is why it fills the copper where it overlaps “GND_top”. You set the order with the arrow buttons at the bottom-left of the list, dragging a zone up or down, or you let KiCad set it for you with the automatic-priority button. That button examines each overlapping pair and gives precedence to the zone with more connected pads or vias in the overlapping region; where the two have roughly the same number, the smaller zone gets the higher priority. When you change a setting here, the preview updates on demand so you can check the result, and no change is applied to the board until you click “OK”, at which point the zones refill if you have asked them to. For a board with one or two pours you may never open it. As soon as a board has several, the Zone Manager saves a lot of time.

Your board now has copper in every area that needs it, and no copper in the areas that must stay clear. The pour sits on a ground net that carries the motor current back to the power supply, it uses the pad connections that suit your assembly method, and it stops at every rule area you drew. The design itself is essentially finished. What it does not yet have is a list of every part on it, with the supplier codes and part numbers someone needs in order to buy the parts and build the board. That list is the bill of materials, and you will build one in the next chapter.

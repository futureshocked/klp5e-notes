3D model

A three-dimensional description of a component’s true shape and height, usually a STEP file, that a footprint can reference. With a model attached to each part, the whole board can be viewed as a solid object, which makes a part facing the wrong way or two parts colliding easy to spot before manufacture. See Vol 1, Part 4.

Annular ring

The ring of copper pad surrounding a plated through-hole. Its width is the difference between the pad diameter and the hole diameter, and a fab house sets a minimum it can reliably produce. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Bill of materials (BOM)

A list of every component a board needs, with the quantity of each, generated from the schematic. It is what a builder orders parts against, and what a factory reads when it assembles the populated board for you. See Vol 1, Part 10.

Boost converter (step-up converter)

A switching power circuit that produces an output voltage higher than its input, by storing energy in an inductor and releasing it in pulses. Also called a step-up converter, it lets a low or falling source, such as a single cell or a small solar panel, power electronics that need a higher, steady voltage. See Vol 1, Part 9.

Copper pour

A large filled area of copper on a layer, rather than a thin trace, typically used to spread a ground or power connection across a wide region of the board. Also called a copper fill or zone. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Cross-probing

Selecting an item in one editor so that KiCad reveals the matching item in another: a symbol in the schematic and its footprint on the board correspond, and selecting either highlights the other, with the 3D viewer participating too. It works because the schematic and board share the same nets and references. See Vol 1, Part 11.

Design Rules Check (DRC)

The layout editor’s check for physical faults against manufacturing limits, such as insufficient clearance between copper features, a track touching the wrong pad, or a hole too close to the board edge. It tests physical reality, the counterpart to the ERC’s logic check. See Vol 1, Part 4.

Design for manufacturing (DFM)

Designing a board to stay within the limits a factory can build reliably: minimum trace width and spacing, minimum annular ring, smallest practical drill size, and copper-to-edge clearance. KiCad’s default design rules already sit well inside what an ordinary fab can make. See Vol 1, Part 10.

Drill file

A plain-text file, separate from the Gerbers, listing every hole a board needs and the diameter of each, covering the through-hole pads, the vias, and the mounting holes. A manufacturer needs it alongside the Gerbers to know where to drill. See Vol 1, Part 10.

Electrical Rules Check (ERC)

The schematic editor’s check for logical faults in a circuit, such as a floating pin, an undriven power net, or two outputs wired together. It tests the design’s electrical intent, not the physical layout. See Vol 1, Part 4.

Fab house

A company that fabricates bare printed circuit boards from a designer’s exported files, and often assembles the components onto them as well. Online fab houses make small prototype runs cheaply and ship them within days to weeks. See Vol 1, Part 10.

Footprint

The physical land pattern a component solders to on the board: the pads, courtyard, and silkscreen outline that represent one part’s footprint in copper and on the surface layers. A symbol is the schematic representation of a part; its footprint is the physical one. See Vol 1, Part 4.

FR-4

The fiberglass-reinforced epoxy laminate used as the substrate for the great majority of printed circuit boards. It is inexpensive, mechanically strong, and a good electrical insulator; the standard board thickness is 1.6 mm. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Gerber

The industry-standard file format for sending a board to a manufacturer. A design exports as a set of plain-text Gerber files, one per layer, describing exactly where the copper, soldermask, silkscreen, and holes go. See Vol 1, Part 10.

Keep-out area

A region of the board where the design forbids placement or copper, used to reserve space for a mounting screw, a connector, or another mechanical feature. The design rules check enforces the restriction during layout. See Vol 1, Part 4.

Mounting hole

A hole used to fasten a board to an enclosure or surface, typically with a screw and nut. Unlike a component hole, it carries no electrical connection and is usually not plated. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Net

A single electrical connection in a design: the set of pins that are joined together and so must sit at the same potential. The schematic declares a circuit’s nets; the layout must route copper to honour every one of them. See Vol 1, Part 4.

Net class

A named group of nets that share routing properties such as track width and clearance, applied to every net in the group rather than set track by track. Net classes let a design express a rule like “all power nets are wider” in one place. See Vol 1, Part 11.

Pad

An exposed area of copper, sized to a component’s connection, onto which that component is soldered. Through-hole pads surround a plated hole; surface-mount pads are flat areas on the board surface. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Panel

A larger manufacturing sheet that carries many individual boards together, separated after fabrication. Combining many small boards onto shared panels is what makes low-cost prototype services inexpensive. See Vol 1, Part 4.

Paste stencil

A thin metal sheet with an opening over each surface-mount pad, used to deposit solder paste onto the right places in a single pass. Often called simply a stencil. See Vol 1, Part 4.

Pick-and-place

The automated assembly step in which a machine positions surface-mount components onto their pasted pads before reflow. The name also refers to the machine itself and to the file that tells it where each part belongs. See Vol 1, Part 4.

Ratsnest

The thin straight guide-lines the layout editor draws between pins that belong to the same net but are not yet routed. Each line marks a connection still waiting for copper; routing replaces it with a track, and the ratsnest shrinks until none remain. See Vol 1, Part 4.

Silkscreen

The printed layer of text and line art, usually white, on the outside surfaces of a board. It carries no electrical signal; it labels components, pins, and the board’s name and version for the people who build and use it. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Solder paste

A grey mixture of tiny solder particles and flux, applied to surface-mount pads before components are placed. Heating it in a reflow oven melts the particles into finished solder joints. See Vol 1, Part 4.

Soldermask

The thin polymer layer applied over the copper to prevent oxidation and confine solder to the exposed pads. Usually green, but available in many colours. Openings in the mask define where solder can flow during assembly. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Substrate

The rigid insulating core that gives a PCB its strength and shape, and onto which the copper layers are bonded. Most boards use a fiberglass-and-epoxy laminate called FR-4. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Surface finish

The thin protective coating applied to a board’s exposed copper pads to keep them solderable and stop them oxidising. Common finishes are lead-free HASL, ENIG (gold over nickel), and OSP. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Surface-mount (SMD)

A component-mounting style in which a part sits flat on the board and solders directly onto surface pads, with no leads passing through the board. Surface-mount parts are smaller and dominate modern electronics. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Symbol

The schematic representation of a component, showing its function and electrical connections rather than its physical shape. A symbol is paired with a footprint, which represents the same part physically on the board. See Vol 1, Part 4.

Thermal relief

A pad-to-copper-pour connection made through a few narrow spokes rather than solid metal, so the surrounding copper cannot pull heat away during soldering. It makes a pad joined to a large pour practical to solder by hand. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Through-hole (THT)

A component-mounting style in which a part’s leads pass through plated holes in the board and are soldered on the far side. Through-hole parts are larger and comparatively forgiving to hand-solder. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Trace

A copper line that carries current between points on a board, the printed equivalent of a wire. Its width is set in the design and determines how much current it can safely carry. Also called a track. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Via

A plated hole that carries a net from one copper layer to another. Through-hole vias span the whole stackup; blind and buried vias connect only a subset of layers on boards that support them. See Vol 1, Part 1.

Design for Manufacturing

You have a finished design and a bill of materials to build it from. The last question is whether a factory can actually make what you have drawn, and make it efficiently. This chapter answers it. The term ‘Design for manufacturing’, usually shortened to DFM, covers two related practices. First, checking a board against the real limits of the process that will fabricate and assemble it, before you pay for a batch of boards that cannot be built. And, second, shaping the design so that the board is quicker, cheaper and more reliable to build once it is in the factory.

A design that is electrically correct can still be difficult or even not possible to make. There may be a track that is too thin for the factory’s etching1, a hole too small for its drills, or a thermal pad that floods with solder and tilts the chip at reflow. Knowing a few things about PCB manufacturing is useful here, though you surely do not need to be an expert. In this chapter you will learn how to check a board against the limits your fabricator publishes, how to export the manufacturing files and inspect them before you send them, and how to record those limits inside the project so that KiCad enforces them for you. You will also learn about panelisation2 and solder-paste stencils3, the two areas where your design choices determine how efficiently the board can be built. The chapter works through all of it on the capstone, because its exposed thermal pad makes the most important of these checks impossible to ignore.

The checking part of that work is a short sequence of checks, and they are not interchangeable. Each check can reveal a larger set of potential issues to fix or improve compared to the one before it, and each requires a little more effort to run (Figure 6.1). The order is what makes the sequence worth having. A problem caught in the first check is fixed in the editor you already have open, while the same problem caught in the last one sends you back through the re-route, the re-plot and the re-upload before you can check it again. We work down that sequence first, then turn to the shaping half.

Figure 6.1: The DFM check sequence. Each stage is a gate: fix what it reports and run it again until it comes back clean. Then go on to the next one regardless, because a later check sees things an earlier one cannot. Stages 1 and 2 run inside KiCad with nothing exported; stages 3 to 5 work on your plotted Gerbers.

Manufacturability Analysis

The good news is that most of the manufacturability check is something KiCad already does, if you tell it what your factory can do. The board-wide constraints live on the “Constraints” page of “Board Setup”, which is where Chapter 3 set them, and among them is a group of manufacturing limits:

  • Annular ring. The minimum ring of copper around a drilled pad. Ask for less, and a drill that lands a little to one side cuts into the edge of the pad and breaks the connection between the hole and the track feeding it.
  • Drill and hole size. The smallest drill and hole the process allows. A hole below it is not a thing the factory can make with the drills it owns, so the order comes back to you for a change.
  • Hole-to-hole spacing. The closest that two holes may sit. Put a pair closer than this and the wall of material between them can break out while the second one is drilled.
  • Silkscreen text. The smallest text that will print cleanly. Below this size the ink spreads and the letters fill in, and a reference designator you cannot read is no help at all when you sit down to assemble the board.
  • Silkscreen clearance. The minimum gap between one silkscreen item and the next. Two pieces of silkscreen printed into each other are less readable than either would be on its own.

Fill these in from the capability page your chosen manufacturer publishes, or import them in one step if the fabricator supplies a capability file, then run the design rules check (DRC). KiCad flags anything on the board that falls outside what the factory can build. That is your first and most reliable manufacturability analysis, stage 1 of the sequence in Figure 6.1. It makes use of the same DRC you already run, with your fabricator’s real limits in it.

The board in this book is built to NextPCB’s published minimums, so those numbers are already in the project. They arrived in one step, by importing NextPCB’s “Simple” capability file, and here they are in full, so that you do not have to go back for them: a 0.127 mm track, a 0.09 mm annular ring, a 0.48 mm via on a 0.15 mm drill, 0.25 mm from hole to hole, and silkscreen text no smaller than 0.762 mm at 0.125 mm thick. Figure 3.3 shows the “Constraints” page holding exactly those values. NextPCB publishes those limits under the headings “Design Rules” and “PCB Manufacturing” capabilities, at nextpcb.com. Every fabricator has an equivalent page, so find your own manufacturer’s before you set a board up, because these limits vary between factories and between the service levels a single factory offers. In other words, you should not use the numbers you see in this page because your manufacturer may have different minimums.

Two related limits sit on a different page of the same dialogue, “Solder Mask/Paste”, rather than with the constraints above. They are the solder mask and solder-paste expansions, which decide how far those openings grow or shrink relative to the pad underneath. The paste one turns out to matter a great deal on this board, so that page has been left until the section on paste and stencils later in this chapter, where you can see it in Figure 6.13 alongside the reason it matters.

The order in which you run the DFM checks matters. Do the KiCad native check first. With your fabricator’s numbers in it, KiCad catches the great majority of manufacturability problems and gives you the opportunity to fix them quickly within your project. Only then is it worth asking for an external opinion via the external DFM tools. Some manufacturers publish their own free analysis tool that inspects your design and reports problems against that specific factory’s process. NextPCB offers one called HQDFM. Keep in mind that HQDFM is not one program but three: a plugin that runs inside KiCad, a browser tool at nextpcb.com, and an application you install on Windows. Those are stages 2, 4 and 5 in Figure 6.1. They grow more capable in that order, and they also grow more troublesome to run in that same order (especially if, like me, you do not run Windows natively on your computer), which is why you work along them starting with the one that takes least effort to run rather than the one that finds the most problems.

Run all three DFM tools, in the same order, even when the one before came back clean, because the Windows application can find issues that the other two cannot. But you should resist the temptation to skip straight to the Windows application, because most problems can be found by the first two, and catching them there will reduce the total amount of time spent on the DFM workflow significantly. Treat every one of these tools as a source of clues of the validity (or not) of your design, not as a substitute for getting the design right in KiCad. The section “A Closer Look at the DFM Tools” takes each of them in turn, once you have the exported files that two of them need.

Gerber Viewer and Analysis

When the design passes the two checks that run inside KiCad, KiCad’s own DRC and the HQDFM plugin, you turn it into the files a factory actually reads. Those are stages 1 and 2 in Figure 6.1, and they are the last two that need nothing exported. Everything after them works on the Gerber files you are about to plot. As you may remember from Volume 1, KiCad plots one Gerber file per layer from “File”, “Fabrication Outputs”, “Gerbers”. Alongside the Gerbers you generate a drill file, which lists every hole and its size, from “File”, “Fabrication Outputs”, “Drill Files”. Ask KiCad to write a Gerber job file too, the small companion file with a .gbrjob extension, because it bundles the stackup and finish information that helps the factory set your board up correctly.

You should never send Gerbers you have not looked at. KiCad includes a separate application, the ‘Gerber viewer’, that opens your plotted files and shows you the board exactly as the factory will receive it, layer by layer, with nothing hidden and nothing assumed. This is stage 3 in Figure 6.1, and it is the first check that works on the exported files rather than on the design. Open your Gerbers there, move through the layers one at a time, and confirm four things:

  • The copper is where you meant it to be.
  • The solder mask opens over the pads you expect.
  • The silkscreen is legible and clear of the pads.
  • The drill file hits every hole.

You met this viewer in Volume 1, and the way into it is the same. It is its own application rather than a mode of the board editor, so you start it from the KiCad project window, either from the “Gerber Viewer” entry in the launcher list down the left (❶ in Figure 6.2) or with Ctrl+G. Load the plotted layers from “File”, “Open Gerber Plot File(s)”, then the drill data from “Open Excellon Drill File(s)” just below it, so that the holes sit on top of the copper and you can see whether each one lands where a pad is. Everything after that happens in the “Layers Manager” on the right (❷). Its “Layers” tab lists every file you have loaded, each with a visibility checkbox and a colour swatch, and an arrow marks the active layer, the one that the layer information and the highlighting tools work on. Its “Items” tab controls the grid, the D-codes and negative objects. Hide the lot, then bring the layers back one at a time. That is how the four checks above actually get made, because a layer viewed on its own is the only way to see what is really on it.

Figure 6.2: KiCad’s Gerber viewer, started from the launcher entry in the project window (❶), with the capstone’s eleven plotted files loaded. The “Layers Manager” (❷) lists them all: two copper layers, two solder mask layers, two silkscreens, two paste layers, the board outline, and the two drill files, plated and non-plated. The canvas is showing the region around the DRV8833, where the exposed thermal pad and its via array sit under the solder mask layer that is currently active.

This is your chance to catch the mistakes that a schematic-level tool cannot see, because you are now looking at the output itself rather than the design that produced it.

One practical note about the drill file is that KiCad can write its drill data in more than one style, and a factory’s ordering system may prefer a particular one. Generate a single combined drill file, and choose the decimal-format option so that every coordinate carries an explicit decimal point. That is the combination that most ordering systems parse reliably. The broader lesson is to read your chosen manufacturer’s ordering guide before you export, and match your output to what they ask for. Your fabricator is the authority on the files they want. The capstone walks through generating and ordering these files against a real manufacturer; here the point is simply that you view them, and check them, before they leave your machine. And once you have looked at those files yourself, they are exactly what the external checkers want from you, which is where the next section picks up.

A Closer Look at the DFM Tools

You now have both halves of what an external check needs: a design that passes KiCad’s own DRC, and a set of plotted files you have looked at with your own eyes. This section takes NextPCB’s three DFM tools in turn, in the order you should run them, starting with the one that takes the least effort.

The Plugin

The plugin is the one to reach for first. Install it the way you install any other plugin in KiCad, from the Plugin and Content Manager you met in Volume 1. The manager opens from the main KiCad project window rather than from inside an editor: click “Plugin and Content Manager”, the last entry in the launcher list down the left of that window, under the description “Manage downloadable packages”. Ctrl+M does the same thing. With the manager open, make sure the repository selector at the top is on the official KiCad repository (❶), and type dfm into the search box to narrow the list (❷). The entry you want is called “HQ DFM” (❸). Select it, click “Download” to queue it (❹), and then “Apply Pending Changes” to install it (❺), as in Figure 6.3. The package is a couple of megabytes and the manager tells you plainly whether it is compatible with the KiCad you are running.

Figure 6.3: Installing the HQ DFM plugin from the Plugin and Content Manager. Choose the official repository (❶), search for dfm (❷), select the “HQ DFM” package (❸), queue it with “Download” (❹), and commit with “Apply Pending Changes” (❺).

Once it is installed, a button for it appears in the PCB editor’s top toolbar (❶ in Figure 6.4). That button is the whole point of this tool. Click it with your board open and the plugin’s window opens over the canvas; press “DFM Analysis” (❷) and it reads the board you have in front of you. Nothing is exported and nothing is uploaded, so the loop from “run the check” to “see the answer” is a few seconds long.

Figure 6.4: The HQ DFM plugin at work on the capstone. Its toolbar button lives in the PCB editor (❶), “DFM Analysis” runs the check (❷), and each row of the report carries a “Check” button (❸) that opens the detail for that problem (❹) and walks you through its occurrences, highlighting each one on the board as you go (❺).

What comes back is a table, one row per thing it knows how to measure. The smallest trace, pad size and spacing, hole diameter and ring, drill-to-copper and copper-to-board-edge, holes on SMD pads, missing solder mask openings, drill hole density, surface finish, and a dozen more. Rows that pass say so and report nothing further. Rows that fail carry a “Check” button (❸), and this is the part I like: pressing it opens a detail window for that specific problem (❹) that lists every occurrence by layer with a count, and clicking an occurrence highlights it on the board underneath (❺). You are never left holding a warning with no idea which feature caused it.

There are three things I want to note here. The first is that the tool is coarse. Its usefulness is the speed with which it shows your potential issues, so that you can decide whether anything needs fixing and, if it does, fix it there and then. Figure 6.4 is a good example. The plugin has flagged the smallest-trace rows on the capstone, and the detail window shows why: the board mixes its track widths, from the 1.5 mm motor-supply tracks and the 0.8 mm neck-down through a group at 0.3 mm to the 0.25 mm logic tracks. That mixture is exactly what you built in the board-setup chapter, wide where the current is and narrow where it is not, and the narrowest width in it is still comfortably clear of NextPCB’s 0.127 mm minimum. So this one gets read, understood and left alone. That is a different act from ignoring it: you looked at the number before you decided, which is the only way a warning can properly be dismissed.

The second note is that the plugin measures against NextPCB’s process, so a “problem” it reports is a problem for that factory, which may not be a problem for another. Third, parts of its interface, the longer rule explanations in particular, are not fully translated into English. Numbers and layer names are clear enough, but do not be surprised to meet a paragraph of Chinese in a rule description.

The Online Viewer

The second form is a browser tool, and it is a Gerber viewer with the DFM analysis layered on top. It renders your board the way the Gerber viewer on your own machine does, then measures it and marks up what it finds, so you get the picture and the verdict in one place. The check itself goes deeper than the plugin’s.

It costs you a little more to run, which is why you use it later in the process. You have to hand it your files, either the Gerbers and the drill file you plotted earlier or the .kicad_pcb file itself, which the site will plot for you. That is a step you did not have to take for the plugin. But you generated and inspected those Gerbers in the previous section anyway, so by the time you get here the files are ready with little additional effort.

Once the board is up, the page looks like Figure 6.5. A layer list runs down the left (❶), and you open, close and hide layers there exactly as you would in KiCad’s own viewer, which is what makes this a genuine second look at the output rather than a report about it. The analysis sits on the right (❷). Each check reports either “Normal” or “Abnormal”, and underneath them the tool lists the parameters it measured off your board: the layer count, the smallest trace width and spacing, the drill and slot sizes, the board dimensions, the drill hole density. An “Errors Only” toggle strips the passing rows away when the list gets long.

Figure 6.5: The capstone in NextPCB’s online Gerber viewer, with the DFM analysis running alongside it. The layer list (❶) works like the one in KiCad’s viewer, the panel on the right (❷) carries the check results and the measured board parameters, and “Download Report” (❸) saves the whole analysis as a document.

That “Download Report” button (❸) is worth pressing. What you get is a standalone document, shown in Figure 6.6, and it is more useful than the on-screen panel in two ways. It renders the top and bottom of the board so that you can see what was actually checked, and it lays every check out in a table grouped by area, with the number of checks run and how many passed or failed. It also states the board specs it derived, such as the trace width and spacing in mils and the proportion of the board taken up by surface finish. Keep the report with the project; when you come back to the board in six months, it tells you exactly what was checked and what the answer was.

Figure 6.6: The downloaded DFM report for the capstone. Rendered top and bottom views on the left, the board specs the tool derived, and on the right every check grouped by area with its pass and fail counts.

Now compare that report against what the plugin reported in this chapter. This will help you understand the capabilities of the two tools. The plugin looked at holes on SMD pads and reported no errors. The online check looked at the same thing, and called it “Abnormal”. There are two failures under “Drill On Pad”, alongside fourteen under “SMD Pad Spacing” and one under the solder mask analysis. It is the same board checked by the two tools at the same time, but the online DFM tool check performed a deeper check, and returned a list of problems the first tool did not see. This is precisely why a clean result is not a reason to stop.

One more thing the report does, which the plugin does not, is that it comments on the board as a whole rather than only on its features. On the capstone it notes that the board is small enough to make surface-mount assembly awkward, and suggests either adding a process edge or putting more copies in the panel. I return to that suggestion in the section on panelisation later in this chapter, which is where both of those terms are explained.

The Desktop Application

The third form is a program you download and install. It is the most capable of the three by a clear margin. It is available on Windows only, and it requires a download and an installation before you can use it, so it is an additional overhead. If you don’t use Windows on your computer, you should consider a virtualisation application like VMware, to allow you to run Windows as a guest operating system, and then the HQDFM application on it (this is my route). Download it from nextpcb.com.

Because I work on Linux, my own routine has settled into two passes. I run the plugin first, inside KiCad, and fix whatever it finds while the board is still in front of me. Then I go straight to the desktop checker, which I keep in a Windows virtual machine for exactly this purpose. The first pass is cheap and clears out most of the list. The second pass is the one I trust to be complete.

What you give it is the same set of files you plotted earlier. The application opens a full computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) viewer with the analysis built into it (Figure 6.7). The layer list down the left (❶) holds every file the tool read out of your export, each with a visibility box and a colour swatch, so that you can isolate a single layer here exactly as you did in KiCad’s own viewer. The analysis fills the panel on the right (❷), and the canvas between them (❸) is where any result you click gets highlighted. Down in the bottom right the tool prices the board and offers to order it, which is worth knowing about in advance so that it does not take you by surprise. This is a fabricator’s application, and it is built to end in an order.

Figure 6.7: The HQDFM desktop application with the capstone’s plotted files loaded. The layer list (❶) works like the “Layers Manager” in KiCad’s Gerber viewer, the analysis panel (❷) carries the two tabs and the measured results, and the canvas (❸) shows the board those results were measured from. The quote and the “Order Now” button sit below the panel on the right.

Notice that the panel on the right carries two tabs, “PCB Analysis” and “PCBA Analysis”. They divide the work the way a factory divides it, between making a bare board and building a populated one. PCB analysis looks at what the fabricator makes. The copper, the drilled holes, the solder mask, the silkscreen and the outline. Everything it needs is already in the Gerbers and the drill file, and it is the tab shown in Figure 6.7. PCBA analysis, where the A stands for ‘assembly’, looks at the same board with its components fitted, and that is a question your Gerbers cannot answer. It requires the bill of materials you learned about in Chapter 5, which tells it which part belongs in each position, and a ‘position file’, also called a centroid or pick-and-place file, which gives the coordinates and the rotation of every part. Given those two, it checks your footprints against a parts database and cross-checks the two files against each other.

Which tab matters to you depends on what you are buying. The capstone is ordered as a bare board and assembled by hand, so the PCB tab is the one this section works through, and the same split appears again in the quote below it, where the bare board and the assembled board are priced separately. If you choose to pay a factory to populate a board for you, the PCBA tab becomes the more important of the two.

Now press “DFM Analysis” (❶ in Figure 6.8) and let it run. On the capstone it comes back with a row that neither of the earlier tools had raised: “Signal Integrity”, marked “Error(s) detected”. Press the “Check” button beside it (❷) and a detail window opens (❸) holding a single entry, “Acute Angle Traces (1 inst., 9.84mil)”. Click that instance and the tool highlights it on the canvas (❹).

Figure 6.8: The acute-angle finding on the capstone. “DFM Analysis” runs the check (❶), the “Signal Integrity” row reports an error and offers a “Check” button (❷), the detail window names the single instance and explains the rule behind it (❸), and clicking the instance highlights the offending corner on the canvas (❹).

What it has found is one corner. The nFAULT track leaves the DRV8833, runs down the board, and turns to reach R1, the resistor that pulls it up to VINT. That turn is a right angle. Every other corner on this board is a 135 degree join, because that is what KiCad’s router produces when it is left in its default 45 degree corner mode, and this one corner is the exception. The 9.84 mil in the report is that track’s width, 0.25 mm, which is enough to find it without hunting.

Two separate considerations sit behind the rule, and they are worth separating, because only one of them is really about manufacturing. The first is etching. A sharp inside corner can hold etchant after the rest of the board has drained, so the copper in that corner is etched for longer than the copper around it and the track can thin there. That is called ‘acid trap’4, and it is the reason this rule exists at all. It is also far less of a threat than it once was, because process control has improved, and it becomes a genuine risk at angles well below 90 degrees rather than at 90 itself. The second issue is electrical. Copper measured across the diagonal of a corner is wider than the track on either side of it, so the impedance changes over that short distance and a fast edge can reflect from it. On a high-speed line that is a real effect. Here it does not matter, because nFAULT is a status line held high through a resistor, and nothing on it moves quickly.

This corner would almost certainly have been fabricated without trouble and worked. I fixed it anyway, and the reason is worth stating, because it is not the same reason that applied to the smallest-trace warning the plugin raised earlier. That warning called for a judgement: I had to look up the narrowest width on the board, compare it with NextPCB’s minimum, and satisfy myself that the difference was comfortable. The judgement cost more than the warning did, and the answer was that nothing needed changing. This corner is the opposite case. The fix is a single drag of the track in the PCB editor, which costs less than deciding whether the corner is worth fixing, so there is no reason to spend the time deciding. You can put the router in its default 45 degree corner mode, drag the corner, and it becomes two 135 degree joins with a short diagonal between them (Figure 6.9), which is the same shape as every other corner on the board.

Figure 6.9: The repaired corner. The nFAULT track leaves pin 8 of the DRV8833, runs down the board, and now reaches R1 through a short diagonal rather than a right angle. The arrow marks the joint that Figure 6.8 highlighted.

Now consider what this finding means for the sequence. The online report a few pages ago has a “Signal Integrity” row of its own, in its PCB_SIGNAL group, and that row came back green. It is the same board, the same plotted files and the same heading, and the two tools give opposite answers. KiCad’s own DRC would never have raised it either because KiCad has no design rule that could. Its constraints cover clearances, track and ring widths, hole sizes and edge distances, and nothing in the list describes the angle at which two segments meet. You could spend a lot of time and effort to write custom rules without expressing this one. It is a design practice that cannot be stated as a design-rule constraint, which is precisely why a tool that measures the plotted copper can see it while a tool that reads your design intent cannot.

There is also a wider lesson to learn here. I drew this board, routed it, ran the DRC over it, and looked at it in three separate viewers earlier in this chapter, and I did not see that corner. That is not unusual, and it is not really a story about carelessness. However carefully you work, a design of any size keeps a few small mistakes in it, and they survive precisely because they are small. A right-angle corner may not draw the eye when you are busy with many other details. It breaks no rule (especially in this example board), raises no DRC marker, and sits among a hundred corners that are perfectly fine. Your attention goes to the difficult parts of a board, which is exactly why small mistakes survive in the easy parts.

That is why making these tools a routine step in every design rather than something you reach for when you suspect an obscure problem. Their value is not only that they look deeper than you can. It is that they look at everything with the same attention, including the parts you stopped looking at a long time ago.

One last note on coarseness, since the previous section made the same point about the plugin. Once you know the shape, you can find a second right-angle corner on this board, on the wide VM track near the connector, and this report does not list it. I have left it there. A right angle on a wide track carrying direct current is not worth a re-route, and the omission is a useful reminder that these tools hand you clues rather than a complete list.

Running Them in Order

The rule that ties the three together is the one worth carrying away, and it is a little counter-intuitive. A clean result from one tool is not permission to skip the next. This happened to me twice on the capstone. A check the plugin had passed came back as a failure once the online tool ran it, with a further fifteen failures beside it that the plugin never raised at all. Then a check both of the first two had passed came back as a failure in the desktop application, on a corner that no design rule in KiCad could have described. I run all of them, every time, whatever the previous one reported, because a clean result from the plugin tells you only that the plugin found nothing.

So why not go straight to the most capable one and save the effort? It may ultimately come down to how you want to work, but for me, it has to do with the effort required. The cost of a DFM problem is not the checking, it is the fixing.

A fix requires a return to the layout editor, the re-route, the re-plot, the re-upload. Every problem you catch in KiCad’s own DRC or in the plugin is a problem you fix in the editor you already have open, with nothing to export and nothing to send. Starting with the simplest tool means the most capable one has less left to find, and the little that it does find is the part that was genuinely worth the extra work.

Run them on the designs you are confident about as well, and this is the part that takes some discipline. It is tempting to treat a DFM check as something you reach for when a board looks risky, and to skip it on the small, familiar, obviously fine one.

But the acute-angle corner was not an obscure problem on a difficult board. It was a trivial mistake on a simple one, and it survived four rounds of my own attention because there was nothing about it to notice. That is the kind of mistake these tools mostly catch: a single feature that breaks no rule, raises no marker, and sits among a hundred identical features that are all perfectly fine. Which means the boards you are least worried about are exactly the ones where a check still pays.

One last thing, since this chapter has leaned on one manufacturer’s tools throughout. Every DFM tool speaks for the factory that wrote it, and its limits are that factory’s limits. If you order elsewhere, find the equivalent tool for the process that will actually build your board. The tool changes; the habit does not, and the habit is what this section is really teaching: check early, check with the simplest tool first, check every time, and escalate.

Panelisation Concepts

If you have ever wondered how a factory makes a hundred small boards efficiently, the answer is ‘panelisation’. A panel is one larger rectangular board that carries many copies of your design, or several different designs, grouped together. It moves through fabrication and assembly as a single unit, and the individual boards are separated from it at the end. Small boards are panelised because handling them one at a time is slow and wasteful, and because the machines that place and solder the parts are built to hold and populate a panel of a workable size. The capstone is the sort of board this applies to, which is what the DFM report was telling you when you ran the online check.

Figure 6.10 shows what that would look like here. Your 42 by 30 mm design on the left, and on the right a panel of nine copies of it. Running around the outside is a plain strip of bare board called a ‘rail’, or a ‘process edge’, which is the term the report used. The rail is there for the machines rather than for you. It gives the conveyor and the placement head something to grip that is not your circuit, it stiffens the panel so that it does not sag, and it carries the two features the assembly line needs in order to locate the panel. The first is a set of ‘fiducials’, small copper dots that a camera uses to work out exactly where the panel is sitting. The second is a pair of ‘tooling holes’, which locate the panel on a fixture. Fiducials are placed asymmetrically on purpose, so that a panel loaded the wrong way round is spotted rather than populated backwards.

Figure 6.10: The capstone on its own, and the panel a factory would build it in. The panel is drawn at half the scale of the single board. Nine copies sit inside a rail that carries the fiducials and tooling holes, the columns are separated by routed slots bridged by tabs, and the rows are separated by V-score lines that run the full width. The two details below show what those joints look like close up: the mouse bites drilled through a tab, and the pair of grooves that make up a V-score, drawn in section through the thickness of the board.

Inside that frame, every board has to be held firmly enough to survive assembly and released easily enough afterwards. There are two ways to do it, and a panel often uses both. The first is to route a narrow slot along the joint, leaving a few short ‘tabs’ of material bridging it, then drill a row of small holes through each tab where it meets the board. Those perforations are the ‘mouse bites’. They weaken the tab along a line, so that the board twists free and leaves a small stub behind that you file flat. Routing works on any outline, straight or curved, and it is the only option where something overhangs the board edge. The capstone is that case, because J1 is a right-angle header whose pins project past the left edge, so its copies need a gap beside them and cannot simply butt together.

The second way is ‘V-scoring’. A pair of angled cutters grooves the panel along a straight line, one from the top and one from the bottom, leaving about a third of the thickness as a web in the middle. Bend the panel and it snaps cleanly along the groove. V-scoring is quicker and cheaper than routing and it wastes no material, but it has two restrictions. The first is that the cut is a straight line running the full width of the panel. The second is that the boards on either side of it have to meet edge to edge, with no gap at all between them. That makes it right for the horizontal joints on this panel, where nothing overhangs and the rows sit directly against each other, and wrong for the vertical ones.

For a board like the capstone, panelisation is mostly something you can let the factory handle. KiCad 10 does not panelise a board for you as a built-in step, and for a first order you rarely need it to. You send your single board, and if you want copies, the manufacturer panelises them as part of the service.

Do not be surprised, though, to find that KiCad can panelise after all. What fills the gap is a plugin rather than a built-in tool, and the best known of them is called KiKit, which you install from the same Plugin and Content Manager you used for the HQ DFM plugin earlier in this chapter. It builds a panel from your board, adds the rail, and places the tabs, mouse bites or V-score lines for you. When you do reach the point of designing your own panel, for a production run or a board you want to assemble in quantity, that is the sort of tool you would reach for, or you would lay the copies out deliberately yourself. Either way it is a Volume 3 concern, and nothing in this chapter depends on it.

What matters for the boards you are designing now is to make them easy to panelise. Keep a small clear margin of empty board around the edge, and keep important copper and parts back from it. You have already done this on the capstone without calling it panelisation, because both ground pours stop one millimetre short of the outline. That margin is what gives the manufacturer who panelises your board room to put a tab or a score line there without cutting into your work, and it matters most at a V-scored joint, where there is no gap at all between your copper and the next board’s. This is the second half of DFM in practice. The board is no easier to check than it was before, but it is quicker and cheaper to build.

Paste and Stencil

The exposed thermal pad you drew back in the footprint chapter is the reason this section matters on this board. Surface-mount parts are soldered by laying a thin, precise layer of solder paste onto their pads, setting the components on top, and heating the whole board until the paste melts, or ‘reflows’, into solid joints. The paste is applied through a ‘stencil’. A stencil is a thin metal sheet with an opening cut over each pad, laid on the board so that a wipe of paste fills the openings and nothing else. Those openings come directly from your design. Every pad has a ‘paste aperture’ on the F.Paste layer, and the stencil is cut from exactly those apertures.

For an ordinary small pad, an aperture the size of the pad is right, and KiCad gives you that by default. The exposed pad under the DRV8833 is where the default is wrong, and this is the lesson. A single large paste opening over that pad lays down so much solder that the chip floats up on the molten paste at reflow. It tilts, forms a poor thermal joint, and can bridge to the pads next to it. The fix is to split the paste opening in two, so that the pad receives the right total volume of paste in a pair of controlled dabs instead of one flood. That choice was made back in the footprint chapter, and it is what makes this board assemble cleanly.

Figure 6.11 is that choice as the factory receives it. This is the Gerber viewer again, with the same eleven files loaded as in Figure 6.2 and every one of them hidden except F.Paste and F.Mask, which is the layer-by-layer method from earlier in the chapter put to work on a single question. The answer is in the middle of the picture: one mask window, and two paste apertures inside it rather than one. That pair of rectangles is what the stencil will be cut from.

Figure 6.11: The DRV8833’s thermal pad in KiCad’s Gerber viewer, with only F.Paste and F.Mask made visible. The orange rectangle is the single solder mask window, the two rounded rectangles inside it are the paste apertures, and the sixteen down the two sides belong to the lead pads.

Figure 6.12 shows the same region with the copper and the drill data switched back on, which puts the two apertures in their proper context. The mask window sits well inside the copper of the thermal pad rather than covering it, and it reaches only the middle two rows of the via grid, leaving the outer rows covered. Seeing the two views one after the other is the habit worth taking from this chapter. A layer on its own tells you what is on it, and a layer seen alongside the others tells you what it will do.

Figure 6.12: The same thermal pad in the desktop checker, with copper, solder mask, paste and drill all visible. The arrows point from the “Top Paste” entry in the layer list to the two apertures it draws. The mask window around them is smaller than the copper pad underneath, and the drilled vias inside it are the middle two rows of the twelve.

What matters is the volume of paste rather than the number of openings you divide it into. Texas Instruments’ own stencil drawing for this package takes the other route and specifies a single opening the size of the mask window, scaled to the thickness of the stencil, while KiCad’s own library for the package splits it as you have. Both are accepted practice, and Chapter 2 worked through the arithmetic that makes the two comparable.

KiCad also lets you adjust the paste openings themselves, and this is the page mentioned at the start of the chapter. Open “Board Setup” and choose “Solder Mask/Paste” from the section list, under “Board Stackup” rather than with the design rules (Figure 6.13). “Solder paste clearance” is the last field on the page, and it shrinks or grows every aperture on the board relative to the pad underneath it. A negative value insets the aperture, which is the direction you normally want, and it can be given as a distance, as a percentage of the pad, or as both together.

Figure 6.13: The “Solder Mask/Paste” page of Board Setup, holding the capstone’s settings. “Solder paste clearance” at the foot of the page is empty, which is the same as zero, so every paste aperture on this board comes out the size of its pad. That is why the split under the DRV8833 had to be built into the footprint back in Chapter 2, rather than set here: this field can only scale every aperture at once. Above it are the solder mask settings, an expansion of 0 mm and a mask-to-copper clearance of 0.15 mm. Read the warning KiCad prints at the top of the page, because it is this chapter’s argument in one line: these are numbers to take from your manufacturer’s specification, and zero is the right answer when you have no specification to work from.

Three levels can set that clearance, and the most specific one wins. A value on an individual pad overrides the one on its footprint, and the footprint’s overrides the board-wide value on this page. That is how a single part gets special treatment without disturbing the rest of the board, and it is why the default here is usually right for signal pads while the thermal pad is the case where you take control.

If you plan to reflow the board you will order a stencil cut from these paste files. If you plan to hand-solder it, you can skip the stencil entirely and flow the pad through its via array by hand, which is the other practical way to build this part.

DFM Rules

Everything in this chapter comes together in one idea. Capture your factory’s limits inside the project, so that the design is checked against them automatically every time you run the DRC, instead of you having to remember them and apply them by hand. The manufacturability constraints from the start of this chapter are the bulk of this, and for many boards they are all you need. When a factory has a limit that the standard constraints do not express, you add a ‘custom rule’ to state it precisely. This is the design-rule mechanism you met in Chapter 3, and typical uses are:

  • A minimum clearance that a particular process demands.
  • A restriction on where copper may sit near the board edge.
  • A check specific to the way this fabricator handles a particular feature.

Here is one that applies to the board in front of you. NextPCB’s capability file put a 0.15 mm floor under the drill size on the “Constraints” page, and that number is the smallest hole the factory can make. It is not the smallest hole you want to pay for. Fine drills wear out quickly and slow the drilling machine down, so most fabricators move a board into a more expensive tier somewhere below 0.3 mm, and a single via you placed without thinking can take the whole order there. What you want is a second floor, your own, sitting above the factory’s. You could of course type 0.3 mm straight into the “Constraints” page, but that page is the record of what the factory guarantees, imported from their capability file, and re-importing that file would quietly reset it. Your own preference is a different kind of statement, and writing it as a rule with a name of your choosing means the DRC report tells you which of the two floors you crossed. This is exactly the gap a custom rule fills.

You do not have to write it from memory. KiCad 10’s graphical rule editor builds a rule from menus and fields, and the route to this one is short. Open it with “Tools”, “Design Rule Editor”, and look in the “Manufacturability” group of the rule tree, which is where the fabrication limits live: the annular ring, the text sizes, the solder mask, and a “Hole” sub-group holding the three constraints that concern drills. Open that sub-group and select “Minimum drill size”. Create a rule under it, name it something you will recognise in a violation report, such as “Preferred minimum drill”, and set its one value to 0.3 mm. That is the whole rule, and what lands in the project says this:

(rule "Preferred minimum drill"
    (constraint hole_size (min 0.3mm))
)

Read that back on the “Custom Rules” page of Board Setup, which is the habit Chapter 3 asks you to form, because the generated text is the only accurate statement of what a rule means. Figures 3.6 and 3.7 show the two editors side by side, working on Chapter 3’s own rule. Then use “Check Rule Syntax” before you close the dialogue, since a rule with an error in it stops the DRC running at all.

Now run the DRC. The new rule reports nothing, because every hole on this board already clears the floor you just set, and the margin is thinner than you might expect. The smallest of them measure exactly 0.3 mm: eleven of the board’s twelve vias, and the twelve in the array under the DRV8833 that you built into its footprint back in Chapter 2. Above those sit the 0.4 mm power via, the six 1 mm holes of the right-angle header, and the 2.5 mm mounting hole. A clean report is the result you want here rather than a sign the rule was wasted. The rule is not there to complain about today’s board. It is there to hold a decision you have already made, so that the hole you add in six months from now, in a hurry, into a crowded corner, cannot quietly move your order into a more expensive tier without telling you.

That is the whole argument of this chapter in one mechanism. Your fabricator’s specification is no longer something you look up in a browser tab. It is part of the project, and the DRC enforces it on every run. A board that is clean against a well-built set of DFM rules is a board you can order with confidence.

By completing this volume, you can author the parts no library has for you, set up a board for the current it carries, pour and relieve its copper, produce a bill of materials that covers more than one build, and check the design against the factory that will make it. What is left is to put the whole set to work on real boards. We start gently, with a warm-up project. Its many repeated parts let you become fluent with layout and reuse before the capstone, where you author the parts from scratch. Let’s build it.

The board is a small dual H-bridge motor driver built around the DRV8833. It is a useful little board, and it is also, not by accident, an ideal teaching tool. A motor driver carries real current, so net classes and copper pours become the difference between a board that works and one that burns up when you use it for the first time. The DRV8833 also ships in a package with an exposed thermal pad, which provides an opportunity to discuss footprints, solder paste, and heat that a simpler part would let you avoid.

You are about to design a board that, in terms of its component count, is smaller than the one you finished in the last chapter. The LED matrix was wide and shallow. It contains lots of components, most of them identical, all of them sourced from a library, where the difficulty was scale. By contrast, the board in this chapter is narrow and deep. It has few parts, one of which you drew yourself, and the difficulty is that the copper has to carry current, and the chip has to shed heat effectively.

Before any of this board reached the PCB editor, I worked out where every part would sit and drew it (Figure 8.1). This is the same habit the warm-up project opened with, and on a board like this one it pays for itself twice over. It tells you what you are about to build, and it is the drawing I keep beside me while I do the layout.

There is one difference from the sketch in Chapter 7 worth naming, because it changes how much you should trust the numbers. That one was a genuine guess, drawn before the design existed, and only a single dimension on it was fixed. This one is not a guess. By the time I drew it the schematic was finished and I had already worked the placement out against the real footprints, measuring each part’s courtyard rather than estimating it, so every number on the drawing is one you can hold me to. That is why the outline reads 52.0 by 48.0 mm rather than roughly fifty millimetres square.

What This Project Teaches You

A capstone is mostly a recital. Most of what follows is you performing skills you already have, and the stages reward those skills rather than teach new ones. Four things on this board are genuinely new, and I will come back to all four at the end of the chapter, once you have done the work:

  1. A part you drew yourself, carrying real current. Chapters 1 and 2 authored the symbol and the footprint as drawings. Here your own footprint has to work as a thermal and electrical path.
  2. The thermal path as a thing you design. The exposed pad, the via field beneath it, and the copper pour it lands on are one system.
  3. Design variants on a real decision. Chapter 5, Bill of Materials, In Depth, taught the mechanism. The current-sense resistors are the first time you have a reason to reach for it.
  4. Custom design rules, and where the dialogue runs out. The fine-pitch chip on this board breaks the custom rule you wrote back in Chapter 3, and rewriting it takes you past what the graphical editor can express.

You do not start from nothing. From the warm-up project you bring net classes with colour and weight on them, so the power section of this board is visible at a glance from the moment you set it up. You want to develop a habit for reading violations, instead of counting them. Some violations are real, others are noise, and over time you will be able to tell them apart. You will also practice precision positioning. Design blocks are the one thing you will not need here, because nothing is repeated on a single-chip board. They come back in Chapter 9.

The board we’re building

The core capstone is a two-layer board that drives two DC motors from a single DRV8833. The part is the ‘DRV8833PWP’, a 16-pin HTSSOP with an exposed thermal pad (I explained why I chose this package, and not the QFN, back in Chapter 2 when we authored its footprint). Around the chip sits everything the datasheet recommends. The motor supply, called ‘VM’, comes in through a bulk capacitor and a P-channel MOSFET1 that protects the board if the supply is connected backwards. Each of the chip’s three supply pins gets its own local bypass capacitor2. A pull-up resistor on the nFAULT line lets the board report a problem, a control header brings out the four logic inputs and the sleep pin, and two sets of screw terminals carry the motor outputs. Add a pair of current-sense resistors3, which we will use later to make use of the design variants feature, and a small indicator LED so that you can see at a glance whether the board has power. Sixteen components in all, on a single schematic sheet.

I chose this board because it is a small yet practical design that gives you the opportunity to practise all the new topics presented in this volume of the book. It needs a custom part, because I would not trust a library footprint I had not drawn myself for a package with a thermal pad, and it needs real net classes, because the motor and supply nets have to carry wide copper while the logic nets are fine on default tracks. It also wants a thoughtfully poured and thermally-relieved ground, a real bill of materials with supplier part numbers, and a manufacturing check, because the thermal pad makes the solder-paste question unavoidable. A simpler board would let you skip at least one of those. This one does not.

Schematic: The Canonical Eight-Step Pass

You know this workflow. It is the eight-step schematic pass from Volume 1’s Chapter 5, and the same one we used for the LED matrix:

  1. Create the project and schematic. Create a directory for the design and open the editor.
  2. Place symbols. Drop a symbol for each part in the circuit.
  3. Annotate. Give every part a unique reference designator.
  4. Set component values. Tell each part what it actually is.
  5. Wire and label. Connect the pins, and name the connections that matter.
  6. Assign footprints. Tell each symbol which physical part it will become.
  7. Electrical rules check. Ask KiCad to find your mistakes while they are still free to fix.
  8. Hand off to layout. Save, and pass the design to the PCB Editor.

The next section explains where every one of those numbers comes from, including why there are three classes and not two. For now, notice two things about the patterns.

The first is that a pattern is a wildcard match, not a name. *VM* has a wildcard at both ends. This board has a net called VM_IN, upstream of the protection MOSFET, and *VM would mean “ends with VM” and would miss it. The net carrying the most current on the whole board would then sit on the Default class at 0.25 mm, and nothing anywhere would tell you.

KiCad will show you what a pattern catches. Select it, and the panel to the right of the pattern list fills with the nets it currently matches. Right now that panel is empty for all four patterns because you have not drawn anything yet. Come back to this page when the sheet is wired, select *VM*, and count what appears. The list should contain two nets.

The second is that Power and Sense have their graphic columns filled in, not left blank. You met this in the warm-up project, where the power rails came out violet at 10 mil. Do the same here, and give Sense its own colour at a weight between the two. When you have finished wiring, the sheet shows you the current path by line weight alone, drawing a heavy wire for the motor supply and the bridge outputs, medium for the two sense returns, and thin for everything a controller drives.

The arrangement follows from the symbol you drew in Chapter 1, which was drawn with this in mind. The two sense resistors go below and right, between the sense pins and ground. Anything arriving from a controller comes in from the left.

R1, the nFAULT pull-up, sits with the control header on the outside of the group. nFAULT is an open-drain output5, which means it can pull its line down but never drive it up, and a resistor to the logic rail is what puts it back up again when there is no fault. Two chips can therefore share one pull-up. Either chip can pull the common line low, and therefore one resistor can serve both. Duplicating R1 with the group would be wrong, so it stays outside from the beginning.

The connectors’ values have changed from the library’s own names to Conn_01x02 and Conn_01x08. The tolerance and the power rating from the table are not on the sheet; these are a purchasing decision, and they belong in the bill of materials.

Three of those values are decisions. These are the bulk capacitor C1, the sense resistors R2 and R3, and the power rating of those resistors. Each one is reasoned from the datasheet, but none of them is a number you can copy straight out of it.

The bulk capacitor, C1, is not one you can find in the datasheet. Section 9.1 (of the datashee) lists what it depends on, which includes the peak current, the inductance of your supply leads, how much ripple you will accept, and whether the motor is being braked, and then says that system-level testing is what settles it. So 100 µF is a conventional judgement for a board in this current class. However, the voltage rating is not a judgement. The same section asks for a rating above the operating voltage, because a decelerating motor pushes energy back into the supply, and VM already reaches 10.8 V. A 25 V part is rated at more than twice that, which leaves room for the spikes a braking motor sends back.

The sense resistors, R2 and R3, come out of section 8.2.2.2 of the datasheet, which gives the chopping current as R = 0.2 V / I and works the example itself. For a 1 A limit, 0.2 Ω. A 1 A limit suits the small motors this board is for, and it sits below the DRV8833’s own 1.5 A rating, so the current limit acts before the chip’s protection does.

Their power rating is not so obvious. Dissipation is I²R, so at the 1 A limit each resistor dissipates 0.2 W, and a 1206 thick-film resistor is commonly rated 0.25 W. That leaves twenty per cent of headroom and no allowance for derating6, and section 8.2.2.3 of the datasheet asks for margin specifically when the board is shared with other components that generate heat, like this board does. So, specify the 0.5 W part.

While we are here, look again at the “1 %” in that table row. It is the resistor’s tolerance, which is a separate specification from its resistance. The 0.20 Ω is the value the part is meant to have, and the 1 % is how far the manufacturer allows the real part to stray from that value, so a resistor out of this batch measures somewhere between 0.198 Ω and 0.202 Ω. Tolerance this tight costs very little on a modern part, and it is worth buying.

However, this 1% does not apply to the current that flows through this resistor. The limit depends on two things. The chip works by comparing the voltage across the sense resistor against a trip voltage7 generated inside the chip, and the datasheet specifies that trip voltage as 160 mV minimum, 200 mV typical and 240 mV maximum, a spread of plus or minus twenty per cent. Divide each of those by 0.20 Ω, and the current limit on any particular chip could be anywhere between 0.8 A and 1.2 A. The resistor is the precise half of a measurement whose overall accuracy is set by the chip. This pairing is common in electronics.

Step 5, wire and label. This is where we’ll have to make decisions, so we will take the sheet in three pieces: the supply path, the driver stage, and the control side.

The supply path runs from J1 through Q1 to the rest of the board, and it contains the first decision (Figure 8.11).

Figure 8.11: The supply input wired, with the first decision visible in the pin numbers. J1 pin 1 carries VM_IN into pin 2 of Q1, which is the drain, and pin 3, the source, leaves as the board’s VM rail. R4 holds the gate at ground on the net named GATE, and C1, along with D1 and its series resistor R5, hangs off VM. The net classes are already doing their work: VM_IN and VM are drawn heavy and violet because *VM* caught them both, while GATE is thin and stays on Default.

Wire J1 pin 1 to Q1’s drain, and take Q1’s source onward as the board’s VM rail. Put R4 from Q1’s gate to ground. Label the incoming side VM_IN and the outgoing side VM.

That is backwards from the way you have seen a P-channel MOSFET wired before, which is deliberate. In the warm-up project, Q1 was a load switch and its source went to the incoming supply. Here the same kind of device is doing a different job, and the difference is the body diode.

Every MOSFET has a diode built into it, formed by the junction between its drain and its body, and in a P-channel device that diode conducts from drain to source. Now follow what happens with the drain on the incoming side. If you connect the supply correctly, then the diode is forward biased8, so the VM rail comes up through it. The gate is then a full VM below the source, which turns the MOSFET hard on, and the channel carries the current at a few tens of milliohms instead of the diode’s several hundred millivolts. If you connect the supply backwards, then the diode is reverse biased and blocks, while the gate and source sit together near ground so that the MOSFET never turns on. Nothing conducts, and nothing is damaged.

If you wire Q1 the other way round, with the source on the incoming side, it will look correct, but it provides no protection. With the supply reversed, board ground is now the highest potential on the board, the body diode is forward biased from the VM rail towards the input, and current flows backwards through every part on the board. The circuit would pass the electrical rules check, pass the design rules check, and work perfectly on the bench until the first time somebody reversed the supply, which is what this arrangement protects against.

There is no Zener diode across the gate, as most reference designs have, because this board does not need it. The gate sits at ground and the source at VM, so the gate-source voltage is simply VM, which reaches 10.8 V at most. The SUD45P03-09 is rated plus or minus 20 V, symmetrically, so there is nothing for a clamp9 to do. Notice that the rating has to hold in both directions: with the supply reversed the gate-source voltage goes positive by up to VM, which is the case people forget to check. On a 24 V board, the same circuit needs the Zener.

The driver stage is the easy part of the sheet, because it is mostly the datasheet’s own application circuit (Figure 8.12). First, wire the three bypass capacitors. C2 from VM to ground, as close to pin 12 as the drawing allows; C3 between VCP and VM, which is the one that surprises people because both of its ends are supply nets; and C4 from VINT to ground. Section 10.1 asks for exactly these three, with these values, “as close to the pins as possible” in each case.

Figure 8.12: The driver stage wired, and now each capacitor sits over the pin it serves. C2 runs from VM to ground, C4 from VINT to ground, and C3 bridges VCP to the VM rail, which is the one with a supply net at both ends. The bridge outputs go out to J2 and J3 in heavy violet, the two sense returns leave through R2 and R3 in the medium weight the Sense class gives them, and both pin 13 and pin 17, the exposed pad, land on ground.

Then wire the outputs. AOUT1 and AOUT2 go to J2, BOUT1 and BOUT2 go to J3. AISEN goes to R2 and BISEN goes to R3, and the other end of each resistor goes to ground. GND on pin 13 and the exposed pad on pin 17 both go to ground.

Pin 17 is the exposed pad, and it must be wired. It is a real pin on your symbol, it carries a net, and it is the only path the heat has out of the package. A schematic that leaves it unconnected will pass the electrical rules check with a warning you can dismiss, but produces a board whose pad has no net, and gives you a chip that overheats under load for reasons that are invisible on the drawing. When you have finished wiring, all seventeen pins must be connected.

The control side holds the last two decisions (Figure 8.13). J4 brings eight signals in from whatever microcontroller drives the board, in this order:

1  VLOGIC    2  GND    3  nSLEEP   4  nFAULT
5  AIN1      6  AIN2   7  BIN1     8  BIN2

Figure 8.13: The control side uses seven labels, a ground symbol and one resistor. Pin 2 gets the symbol rather than a typed GND, for the reason the end of this step gives. R1 runs from nFAULT on pin 4 up to VLOGIC on pin 1, and pin 1 goes nowhere else on the board, which is the whole reason a logic rail is brought in at all. Pin 2 is the only heavy wire in the group, because it is ground and *GND puts it in the Power class; everything the controller drives stays thin.

The DRV8833 has no logic supply pin at all, because it does not need one. Its input thresholds are absolute rather than proportional to a supply, at 2.5 V for nSLEEP and 2.0 V for the rest, so a 3.3 V or a 5 V controller drives it directly with nothing in between.

So why bring a logic rail onto the board?

Because of nFAULT. Being open-drain, it can pull its line low but never drive it high, so it needs a pull-up resistor to reach a logic high. Whichever rail that resistor connects to is the voltage the controller’s input pin sees whenever there is no fault, and it has to be a voltage that controller is built for. A 3.3 V controller must see 3.3 V on the fault line, not 5 V. Three rails on this board could hold that line high, and only one of them is right.

  1. Pin 1 of the header, the controller’s own logic rail. Different controllers run on different logic rails, so this board takes the rail from the controller itself. R1 goes from nFAULT to pin 1 of the header, and pin 1 connects to nothing else on the board.
  2. VINT, on pin 14. Do not! It is tempting because it is a supply that already exists here. The datasheet’s pin table calls VINT an internal supply bypass, and specifies it with a 2.2 µF capacitor to ground. It is a bypass point for the chip’s own internal regulator, not a rail to hang external loads on, and its voltage is not the controller’s either.
  3. VM. This one can destroy the controller. The datasheet looks as if it permits it. Section 7.3.4 says that nSLEEP “can be pulled up to the supply (VM)”. That applies to nSLEEP alone, which has a clamp inside the chip to hold the voltage down. nFAULT has no clamp, so a fault-free board would present up to 10.8 V to a controller input rated for 3.3 V.

Finally, label the nets. Nineteen of them get names, and you have to be careful, because Chapter 9 stamps a second copy of this stage and a design block reproduces its labels exactly as written. That is something you will do in the next chapter. For now, name them as the datasheet names the pins, and use the plain forms: VM_IN, VM, AOUT1, AOUT2, BOUT1, BOUT2, AISEN, BISEN, VCP, VINT, VLOGIC, nFAULT, nSLEEP, AIN1, AIN2, BIN1, BIN2 and GATE. GND is the nineteenth. Use the ground symbol from the power library wherever a pin goes to ground, as you have on every board so far. A typed GND label on a power input pin leaves the rules checker unable to tell that the rail is genuinely fed.

The twentieth net runs between R5 and the LED’s anode, and doesn’t need a name. Nothing in this chapter, in the bill of materials or in Chapter 9 ever refers to it, so KiCad’s own Net-(D1-A) serves perfectly well. Not every net in a schematic needs a name.

Step 6, assign footprints. Most of this is a lookup, and the Symbol Fields Table is again quicker than the assignment dialogue for a board this size (Figure 8.14).

Figure 8.14: The Symbol Fields Table with every footprint assigned. Parts that share a value and a footprint are grouped into one row, which is why H1-H4, J1-J3 and R2, R3 each appear once with a quantity beside them; the triangle at the left of a grouped row opens it. U1’s row is the one to look at, because it is the only line on the board pointing into a library you wrote, and it is also the only line carrying an MPN and a supplier link, which the bill of materials will want later.

U1 and Q1 arrive ready to use, no additional work needed. U1 arrives with its footprint already attached. Q1 arrives with its footprint attached too, because SUD45P03-09 is a real manufacturer’s part in KiCad’s own library and whoever drew it filled in the same field.

The three screw terminals take the same footprint. The rest are ordinary: 0603 for the small resistors and the two small capacitors, 1206 for the sense resistors, 0805 for the LED and the 10 µF, a radial through-hole can for C1, and a 2.54 mm pin header for J4.

Step 7, run the electrical rules check. Aim for zero errors and zero warnings, and expect to get there in two passes rather than one (Figure 8.15).

Figure 8.15: The second pass. “Violations (0)”, and a zero against both Errors and Warnings. Behind the dialogue are the two symbols that took it there, ringed: a PWR_FLAG on the VM rail, and another on GND. As in the warm-up project, the “Ignored Tests (4)” tab beside the violations is worth opening once. The first pass will report that a power input pin is not driven by any power output pin, once for VM and once for GND. This is not a mistake in the wiring. Both rails arrive on this board through a connector, and as far as the checker is concerned a connector pin is not a source of power, so it cannot see anything driving them. Add a PWR_FLAG symbol to each rail and the complaint goes away. Two flags, one on VM and one on GND.

VLOGIC will not complain, because it is an ordinary named net rather than a power net, and nothing on this board is powered from it except R1. Leave it that way.

Step 8, hand off to layout. Save the schematic, and before you open the PCB Editor, do one more thing that is not in the eight steps and should be. Export the netlist and read it.

In the Chapter 7 project, I encountered a defect where a tidy horizontal wire from one driver’s DOUT to the next one’s DIN passed straight through a third pin on its way across, four separate links became one net, and four chips ended up wired in parallel listening to the same signal. The sheet looked correct in the editor and the rules checker said nothing, because that is not something that the rules checker checks for. The ERC has no way of knowing what you meant to draw. The netlist is the only artefact that puts your intention and KiCad’s reading of the sheet side by side, so it is the only place a wire that connects the wrong two things, correctly, will show up.

Export the netlist with “File”, then “Export”, then “Netlist…”, leave the format on the KiCad tab, and save the file into the project folder as net.net. It is plain text and you open it in any editor. Scroll down to the nets segment, which contains one block per net, like this:

(net
    (code "3")
    (name "/AISEN")
    (class "Sense,Default")
    (node
        (ref "R2")
        (pin "1")
        (pintype "passive")
    )
    (node
        (ref "U1")
        (pin "3")
        (pinfunction "AISEN_3")
        (pintype "passive")
    )
)

This block reads like this: the net called AISEN is in the Sense class, and the only two things on it are pin 1 of R2 and pin 3 of U1. The leading slash in /AISEN is the sheet path, which every label you typed picks up because this design has one sheet; GND appears without one because it comes from a power symbol rather than from a label. And the two PWR_FLAG symbols you added in step 7 are nowhere in the file. Neither are the ground symbols. They are virtual parts that exist for the rules checker, and KiCad leaves them out of the netlist entirely, so their absence is correct rather than a missing connection.

This board has twenty nets, and there are three things to look for.

  1. Twenty nets. Nineteen are the names from step 5, and the twentieth is KiCad’s Net-(D1-A) between R5 and the LED. Any additional nets that you do not recognise are almost always one or more wires whose end landed a fraction of a millimetre off a pin, leaving a stub with one node on it.
  2. VM_IN and VM are two nets, not one. /VM_IN should hold only J1 pin 1 and Q1 pin 2, and /VM should hold Q1 pin 3 along with C1, C2, C3, R5 and U1 pin 12. If a stray wire has bridged across Q1, the two collapse into a single net, the reverse-polarity protection is gone, and nothing else in the process will tell you.
  3. All seventeen of U1’s pins should appear only once. Search the file for (ref "U1") and you should get eighteen hits: one in the component list at the top, and seventeen among the nets. Pin 17 is the one to look for by name, because it is the exposed pad, and a pad that is missing here or that sits on a net called unconnected-(U1-PAD-Pad17) is the failure described in step 5, invisible everywhere except this file.

While you are here, look at the class field. This is the only place that proves the four assignment patterns did what you intended when you wrote them into Schematic Setup: /VM_IN reads Power,Default because *VM* matched it, and if the pattern had been *VM instead, it would read Default alone and the highest-current net on the board would be routed at 0.25 mm.

Reading the file straight through once is worth doing because it gives you a complete picture of the schematic. Think of it as the schematic’s source code. After that, the project ships the same tools/checknet.py from Chapter 7, with its assertions rewritten for this board. Run it from the project directory:

python3 tools/checknet.py net.net --nets --u1-pins --classes

Each option checks one of the three things above, and a passing run says so:

  OK   19 named nets + 1 auto-named, exactly the nets step 5 names
  OK   all 17 U1 pins wired exactly once, pad included
  OK   *VM* / *GND / *OUT* / *ISEN resolved every net to its intended class

Run it with no options and it prints every net and the pins on it, one net per line, which is the fastest way to see the whole board at once.

A clean electrical rules check is not the same thing as a correct schematic. Chapter 7 ended on that lesson, and this chapter ends on it too, in a different disguise. Now let’s set up the board.

Board Setup for a Power Board

Open the PCB Editor on the empty board and go to Board Setup before you import anything. The net classes are already there, because you set them in Schematic Setup and the two dialogues read the same settings. What remains is everything that belongs to the board rather than to the circuit: the stackup, the fabricator’s limits, the track and via sizes you want within reach, and the custom rules.

Chapter 3 built all of this against this board, so most of what follows will look familiar. I will try not to repeat what you already know as much as possible, or at least keep repetitions (if necessary) short.

The stackup is two layers, 1.6 mm thick, with 1 oz copper (35 µm) on both. Set it on the “Physical Stackup” page.

The fabricator’s limits go on the “Constraints” page, and rather than typing them, import them with the recipe in Chapter 10, Intermediate Recipes, in the section on importing a fab’s design rules. These are NextPCB’s published minimums, the same set the warm-up project targeted (Figure 8.16).

Figure 8.16: NextPCB’s published limits, imported. Three of the fields are correctly left at zero. Minimum clearance, minimum connection width and the silkscreen item clearance are values for which the fabricator set no board-wide floor. Instead, these minimums will be set in the net classes. The minimum thermal relief spoke count of 2 is one number we will re-examine later.

Each of these numbers is guaranteed by the factory as being within its capabilities. These are not numbers that you should feel obliged to adopt in your design. They are the floor, and your design lives well above it.

Let’s look at the three zeros. A minimum set in the Constraints page is an absolute floor and no custom rule can go beneath it. That is the opposite of how net classes work, where a later, narrower rule wins. As you will see, the custom rule you are about to write relaxes clearance to 0.15 mm around one chip, and it can only do that because minimum clearance here is 0. If you typed 0.20 mm into that field, thinking of it as a sensible default, the custom DRC rule accepting a clearance of 0.15 mm would silently fail to take effect and the board would report violations you could not clear from the rules file. That’s why it’s better to leave the zeros as such in the Constraints page.

The pre-defined sizes are the track and via widths that appear in the router’s dropdown while you work. This board carries four track widths:

0.25 mm   the Default class width, for logic
0.30 mm   the second neck-down, at the chip's pads
0.80 mm   the first neck-down, on the approach
1.50 mm   the Power class width

Two of those are simply the class widths. The other two are the two stages of a ‘neck-down’10, which is a deliberate local narrowing of a track so that a wide run can reach a pad narrower than itself. Chapter 3, Board Setup, introduced the idea in its section on custom rules and gave both numbers; the next section is where they come from.

The custom rules are where this board changes what Chapter 3 gave you. Chapter 3 wrote three, and the first and third stand exactly as they were:

(rule "Power minimum width"
    (constraint track_width (min 0.8mm) (opt 1.5mm) (max 2.2mm))
    (condition "A.hasNetclass('Power')")
)

That one puts a floor under the power tracks, because a net class width is what KiCad routes at and not a limit it enforces. The third rule excuses the connector silkscreen that deliberately runs past the board edge. These rules are correct for our current design.

The second rule needs a rewrite. In Chapter 3 it read like this:

(rule "U1 fine-pitch breakout width"
    (constraint track_width (min 0.2mm))
    (condition "A.NetName == '/AOUT*' || A.NetName == '/BOUT*' || A.NetName == '/VM'")
)

Replace it with this:

(rule "U1 fine-pitch breakout"
    (constraint track_width (min 0.2mm))
    (constraint clearance (min 0.15mm))
    (condition "A.intersectsCourtyard('U1')")
)

Two things changed (Figure 8.17).

Figure 8.17: All three of this board’s custom rules on the Custom Rules page, and “No errors found.” beneath them. That message is the one to check before closing, because a rules file with a syntax error in it is not partially applied, it is ignored altogether, and a board with no custom rules reports no custom violations. Note that the breakout rule carries two constraints, on lines 7 and 8, under the single condition on line 9.

The rule now relaxes clearance as well as width. The pads of the HTSSOP footprint are 0.45 mm wide on a 0.65 mm pitch, which leaves 0.20 mm of bare board between one pad and the next. Run a 0.30 mm track into the middle of one of those pads and its edge comes within 0.275 mm of the neighbouring pad (Figure 8.18). The Power class asks for 0.30 mm of clearance. So every single approach to this chip is a clearance violation, and no amount of careful routing will fix it.

Figure 8.18: Why the relaxation is unavoidable, drawn to scale on U1’s left-hand pads. (1) The Power class’s 1.50 mm does not merely crowd its neighbours, it lands on 0.325 mm of each, so no clearance setting could rescue it. (2) Narrowed to 0.30 mm it fits, and is still only 0.275 mm from each neighbour where the class asks for 0.30 mm, which is why every approach to this chip is a violation on a board that is otherwise perfect. (3) Inside the courtyard, where 0.15 mm applies, the same copper passes, and the run steps down twice on its way in. Notice how little board the relaxation covers: the courtyard reaches only 0.25 mm past the ends of the pads.

There are three ways out of that, and only one of them is good. The first is to drop the Power class clearance to 0.20 mm, which throws away clearance across the whole board to solve a problem that exists in a single location. The second is to set a clearance override on the footprint itself, which works, and hides the decision inside a part that will carry it silently onto every board you ever place it on. Better than either is to write down what you actually decided as a custom rule. This rule codifies your decision that inside this chip’s courtyard, and nowhere else, 0.15 mm is acceptable.

The rule is now aimed at a place rather than at a list of net names. This is the more interesting change. A.intersectsCourtyard('U1') selects any copper that falls inside U1’s courtyard, which is the boundary you drew around the footprint in Chapter 2.

Recall from Chapter 3 that KiCad reads the rules file from the bottom up and stops at the first rule that matches, so this rule overrides the “Power minimum width” rule above it wherever both apply. With the old condition, “wherever both apply” meant every AOUT, BOUT and VM track anywhere on the board. The 0.80 mm floor derived from the nets it was written to protect. A power net left at 0.30 mm would not raise a violation.

Scoping by courtyard fixes that in one step. The relaxation now applies at the chip and stops at the edge of its courtyard, so a neck-down track that runs on across the board is reported. When I made this change on the demonstration board from Chapters 3 to 6, it found four tracks that had stayed narrow well past the point where they needed to.

In the routing section, later in this chapter, we will replace the courtyard in that condition with a region you draw yourself. The region will allow us to mark precicely where the changed rule will apply. Don’t worry about exactly how this will work, as I will explain and demonstrate shortly.

There is another benefit. The old rule named AOUT*, BOUT* and VM, so it needed editing every time the board gained a power net. It had already missed one, VM_IN, on the far side of the protection MOSFET, which was not in the list. A condition that names a place has no list to fall out of date.

That property has a limit, though. This condition names U1, and in Chapter 9 we will add a second driver chip. The fix there is not to extend the condition to U1 and U2, because that represents the same list problem we have seen before. Instead, we will give both chips a ‘component class’, which is a named label you attach to a group of parts, and aim the rule at the class.

Click “Check Rule Syntax” and confirm it reports no errors before you go any further.

One last thing before you close the dialogue, in case you are wondering why you typed that rule instead of clicking it together. KiCad 10 has a graphical Design Rule Editor, on the Tools menu of the PCB Editor. It is a good tool, and you will use it later in this chapter. However, the graphical rule editor could not have written this particular rule (at least at the time I am writing this) because a rule in the graphical editor can carry only one constraint, and this specific rule carries two. In addition, the tool’s condition picker offers nets, net classes and rule areas but not courtyards, so A.intersectsCourtyard('U1') would have been typed as raw syntax anyway. The dialogue is quicker for a rule that captures or describes one parameter about one class of nets, and the text editor is where a rule goes when it says two parameters at once, or aims at a place.

One more interesting “gotcha” I discovered as I was experimenting with the graphical rules editor is how it handles comments. The rules file accepts comments, being any line beginning with #, and a rule like the one you have just written is worth commenting. But the graphical editor does not edit your text. It builds rules from a model and writes the file out again from that model. Any freestanding comments will disappear as a result of using the graphical editor. If you comment your rules, and you should, keep the commented version somewhere the editor cannot reach it, or simply decide that this board’s rules belong to the text editor and leave them there. The project files for this chapter carry the comments.

Now, save Board Setup, and let’s work out where those numbers came from.

Sizing the Power net class

Chapter 3 set out the net classes this board needs and provided the values they carry, but it left unexplained why those values are correct. Let’s look at the “why” now. Two facts help determine every number in the Power class. The first is how much current each net has to carry, which the DRV8833 datasheet tells you. The second is how thick the copper is, which you settled in the stackup, which is 1 oz, roughly 35 microns, on both layers of this two-layer board.

Start with the current, as everything else depends on it. Each H-bridge in the DRV8833 is rated for 1.5 A continuous and 2 A in short peaks, so any one of the four bridge outputs, AOUT1, AOUT2, BOUT1, and BOUT2, sees at most 1.5 A while the motor is running. That 1.5 A is a property of the package as much as of the silicon. The datasheet’s first page lists the same die in three packages: 1.5 A for the HTSSOP you are using and for the QFN, and 500 mA for the plain TSSOP. As you can see, the same chip, containing the same bridges, is rated very differently depending on which package is in use. The only difference between the packages is that the TSSOP has no exposed thermal pad. The additional 1A is due to our chosen package’s pad, the via field under it, and the copper it lands on. The motor supply VM is interesting, because it feeds both bridges at once. If you run two motors hard at the same time, VM carries the sum, 3 A, and that makes it the most demanding net on the board.

Let’s look at the return path next. Current leaving a bridge does not go straight into the ground pour. It goes out of the chip’s AISEN or BISEN pin, through the sense resistor, and only then into ground. So the first part of the return is a track, carrying the full bridge current, and only the part downstream of R2 and R3 is a pour. Those two short runs are the reason this board has a third net class.

Everything else, the four logic inputs, the sleep line, and the fault pull-up, moves a few milliamps and could route on almost any width you like.

Now let us turn that current into a width, and for this we will use a calculator. KiCad ships a standalone application called ‘Calculator Tools’, and its “Track Width” tab is the tool for this job. You give it a current, a temperature rise you are willing to accept, and the copper thickness, and it gives you back the width. I allowed a 10 °C rise, which is the conservative choice and the one I would recommend on any board that is placed in a case, and set the copper to 1 oz. Read the answer from the “External layer” column (not the internal one), because a two-layer board has no internal layers at all. Both of your copper layers are on the outside, where a track radiates heat to the air. On those settings a bridge output at 1.5 A wants 0.53 mm, and VM at 3 A wants 1.39 mm.

Figure 8.19: The VM case worked in Calculator Tools. Enter the current, the temperature rise you will accept and nothing else (❶); the conductor length only affects the resistance and voltage-drop figures below, not the width. Set the copper to 1 oz (❷). Read the answer from the “External Layer Tracks” panel (❸), which gives 1.39 mm. The “Internal Layer Tracks” panel beneath it answers a different question and does not apply to this board: it asks for 3.56 mm because an internal track is buried in resin with nowhere to shed its heat.

A class carries one width, though, and it applies that width to every net you put in it, so the width has to be the one its most demanding member needs. That member is VM at 1.39 mm, which I round up to a clean 1.50 mm. The bridge outputs then get considerably more copper than they strictly need, and that is fine. Wider copper runs cooler, costs nothing on a board this size, and makes the power section easy to distinguish by eye. Sizing the class at the bridge-output figure instead would have been the more elegant-looking choice, and the wrong one, because VM would then be under-copper by default and nothing would tell you. A class width is the width KiCad routes at, not a limit it enforces, so a net that is too thin for its job raises no violation. Size the class for its worst case.

That leaves the two sense nets. AISEN and BISEN are in the motor current path, so the Default class is wrong for them. Put 0.25 mm into the calculator the other way round, as a width rather than a current, and it comes back with about 0.86 A at the same 10 °C rise. These nets see the full bridge current, 1.5 A, which is nearly double that. Leaving them on Default would be wrong.

The Power class is wrong for them too. Its 1.50 mm is sized for VM at 3 A, and the sense nets never carry 3 A; they carry what one bridge carries. The calculator already provided what that needs, when you worked out the bridge outputs (0.53 mm). So the Sense class rounds that up to 0.60 mm, keeps Default’s 0.20 mm clearance, and picks up both nets with the pattern *ISEN.

It would have been simpler to put them in Power and give them 1.50 mm (nothing would have caught fire). I did not, for two reasons. A 1.50 mm track arriving at a 1206 resistor pad is wider than the pad, which is untidy at best. Think of a ‘class’ as a statement about what a group of nets is for, so a class whose members do not share a job is a class that will mislead the next person who reads it (most likely, you!).

The question of clearance can be answered with help from the KiCad calculator. The “Electrical Spacing” tab holds the IPC-222111 table of minimum conductor spacing by voltage (Figure 8.20), and VM on this board tops out at 10.8 V, which lands in the table’s lowest band. Depending on which column of that band applies, a bare external track or a track arriving at a component’s lead, the answer is somewhere between 0.10 mm and 0.13 mm. In other words, the voltage on this board is nowhere near high enough to be what sets your clearance. What actually sets it is manufacturing: your fabricator’s minimum, and the fact that the power nets end at screw terminals and beside a hand-soldered thermal pad, where a wider gap is low-cost insurance against a solder bridge. That is the reasoning behind 0.30 mm on the Power class and 0.20 mm on Default.

Figure 8.20: Minimum spacing from IPC-2221. VM tops out at 10.8 V, so only the top row of the table applies: 0 .. 15 V, boxed here with its column headings (❶). Two of its columns cover this board. B2 is an uncoated external conductor (❷), and the minimum spacing for it is 0.10 mm, which is the gap between bare tracks. A6 is an uncoated external component lead or termination (❸), and its minimum is 0.13 mm, which is the gap where a track meets a pad. The standard’s answer for this board is therefore 0.13 mm. Column B4 is external copper under a permanent coating, which is what solder mask is, and its minimum is lower still at 0.05 mm. This board uses 0.20 mm and 0.30 mm, both well above what the standard requires. Voltage only becomes the deciding factor much further down the table, at 101 V and above, where B3 rises to 3.2 mm.

We should also look at the “Via Size” tab, which works out how much current one plated barrel can carry. Give it the 0.40 mm hole and 0.80 mm pad from the Power class, the 25 micron plating (0.025 mm) a standard process guarantees, and the 1.6 mm board thickness, then enter the 3 A that VM carries and read the “Estimated ampacity” line12 (Figure 8.21). It says 2.31 A, at the same 10 °C rise you sized the tracks for. Those two numbers sit a few centimetres apart in the same dialogue, and they do not agree. One via cannot carry this board’s supply current.

That is worth pausing on, because nothing about a via looks like a bottleneck. The resistance is 0.00082 Ω and the voltage it drops at 3 A is under three millivolts, so electrically the barrel is invisible. What limits it is heat, and there is very little copper in it to move heat with. A 0.40 mm hole plated to 25 microns is a cylinder wall 25 microns thick, which comes to 0.0334 mm² of cross-section.

Figure 8.21: How much current one power via can actually carry. The parameters describe the Power class via exactly, a 0.40 mm hole in a 0.80 mm pad, plated to 25 microns through 1.6 mm of board, with the 3 A that VM carries entered underneath (❶). The answer that matters is “Estimated ampacity” (❷), which reports 2.31 A at a 10 °C rise. The via is being asked for a third more current than it can carry, and the dialogue says so without drawing any attention to it. The resistance above is almost nothing, 0.00082 Ω for 2.5 mV of drop at 3 A, which is exactly why an undersized via raises no complaint you would notice: it does not drop voltage, it gets hot. Notice what the panel does not report. There is no cross-sectional area here and no equivalent track width, so the comparison against your 1.50 mm run is one you make yourself, on the next tab. The last four rows, capacitance through reactance, belong to high-speed work and have nothing to say about a motor supply.

The tab will not put that in the units the rest of this section works in, but the application will if you ask it. Switch to “Track Width”, enter 2.31 A at the same 10 °C rise, and read the “External Layer Tracks” panel (Figure 8.22). It comes back with 0.97 mm13, and with a cross-section of 0.0334 mm², which is the via’s own figure returned to you from the other direction. That is the whole comparison: a via and a track are both just copper with an area, and this via has about two thirds of the copper in the 1.50 mm run feeding it. The track carries 3.17 A comfortably. The via it hands off to carries 2.31 A.

Figure 8.22: The same question asked the other way round: what width of track carries the via’s 2.31 A. That current goes in at the same 10 °C rise (❶), and the “External Layer Tracks” panel answers 0.97 mm of 1 oz copper (❷). Read the cross-section area underneath as well, because 0.0334 mm² is the via’s own barrel area arriving from the other direction, and that match is what makes the comparison legitimate. To IPC-2221 a via and a track are the same thing, a quantity of copper with somewhere to shed its heat. The conductor length of 20 mm affects only the resistance, voltage drop and power loss below it, never the width, so it can be anything. This is also the tab that sized the class in the first place, back at Figure 8.19, so 0.97 mm is directly comparable with the 1.50 mm it settled on.

The fix is simple once you know to look for it. Put two vias in parallel wherever a power net crosses to the other side, which doubles both the copper and the ampacity, to 4.62 A, and the pinch disappears. The exposed pad under the chip is the same reasoning read off a different row. Set the hole to 0.30 mm, the size of the thermal vias you drew in Chapter 2, and the tab reports a thermal resistance of 156 °C/W, which is a poor way out for a chip dissipating watts. Twelve of them in parallel bring that down to about 13 °C/W. That is why the pad carries a field rather than one or two.

A 1.50 mm track cannot arrive at the chip’s pads because there is not enough room. The HTSSOP pads are 0.45 mm wide on a 0.65 mm pitch, which leaves 0.20 mm of bare board between neighbours, so a power track run has to narrow twice on its way in. First is to 0.80 mm on the approach, and then to 0.30 mm for the last hop onto the pad. Both widths are on the “Pre-defined Sizes” menu for that reason.

Those short necks are not problematic. Current-carrying capacity is limited by the length of the narrow section as much as by its width, and a millimetre of 0.30 mm copper on the way into a pad produces only negligible heat. What matters here is keeping them short, which is a placement problem instead of a routing one. This is another reason why placement is important.

The two necks are also why the custom rules are written like they are. “Power minimum width” sets its floor at 0.80 mm rather than at the class width, so that the first narrowing passes and a power net left thin in the open does not. The breakout rule then relaxes both width and clearance inside the chip’s courtyard, so that the last hop passes as well. Between them, the two rules describe the shape of a correct power run on this board. It is wide everywhere it can be, and narrow only where the package forces it.

One more thing to note about the 1.50 mm width for this power track is that it is sized for this board, where VM feeds two bridges and therefore carries at most 3 A. It is not a property of the DRV8833, and it is not a number to carry forward without checking. Chapter 9 puts a second driver chip on a shared supply rail, which makes it four bridges and 6 A, and the same calculator asks for about 3.62 mm at that current. Left at 1.50 mm, the rail would run at roughly a 42 °C rise instead of the 10 °C you designed for. It would still work, but it would be wrong.

Remember that when you copy a project, the original net classes, custom rules and footprints are copied across to the new project, but the numbers they contain are not necessarily correct for the new project. A net class records a decision you made about a particular board, and the next board is a different board.

Chapter 10, Intermediate Recipes, has the step-by-step recipe for all three calculator tabs, in its section on the calculator tools. Work through it once with these numbers in front of you, and the next board you size will take about five minutes.

A 1.50 mm track cannot arrive at U1 because it is too wide to fit. As you may remember from earlier, U1’s pads are 0.45 mm wide on a 0.65 mm pitch, which leaves 0.20 mm of bare board between neighbours, and there is simply nowhere for one and a half millimetres of copper to land. So every Power-class net narrows twice on its way in. It runs at 1.50 mm, drops to 0.80 mm on the approach, and drops again to 0.30 mm for the last hop onto the pad (Figure 8.32). Both intermediate widths are on the “Pre-defined Sizes” menu.

Now the other side of the package, where the same two narrowings have to happen (Figure 8.33). U1’s bottom row carries AOUT1, AISEN, AOUT2, BOUT2, BISEN and BOUT1 across eight pads at 0.65 mm pitch. Keep the narrow sections short. A track’s current-carrying capacity depends on the length of its narrow section as much as on its width. A millimetre of 0.30 mm copper produces very little heat; ten millimetres of it produces enough to matter. About a millimetre is the target, and VM reaches it at 1.03 mm. The six escapes from the bottom row cannot, because nets leaving one row at 0.65 mm pitch have to move apart before any of them has room to widen, so they run 1.56 to 2.01 mm at 0.30 mm. Two millimetres of 0.30 mm copper still gives a temperature rise of a fraction of a degree, so that length is an acceptable cost here. Do not accept it without measuring, though. This is why the placement put the bypass capacitors and the sense resistors as close to their pins as it did. Move them further from their pins and the necks grow with them.

Two custom rules from board setup decide whether this pattern passes DRC. Here they are again, as you typed them into the Custom Rules page:

(rule "Power minimum width"
    (constraint track_width (min 0.8mm) (opt 1.5mm) (max 2.2mm))
    (condition "A.hasNetclass('Power')")
)

(rule "U1 fine-pitch breakout"
    (constraint track_width (min 0.2mm))
    (constraint clearance (min 0.15mm))
    (condition "A.intersectsCourtyard('U1')")
)

“Power minimum width” puts a floor of 0.80 mm under every Power-class track, so the 0.80 mm approach section clears that floor and a power net left thin in the open does not. “U1 fine-pitch breakout” sits below it in the file and therefore wins wherever both apply, and it relaxes width to 0.20 mm and clearance to 0.15 mm for whatever its condition selects. That relaxation is what lets the 0.30 mm last hop onto the pad pass.

The rule is scoped15 by A.intersectsCourtyard('U1'), so both relaxations apply to copper that touches U1’s courtyard and to nothing else. That courtyard is the boundary you drew around the footprint back in Chapter 2. Its edge sits 0.25 mm past the ends of the pads, which puts it 1.00 mm out from the centre of each pad row. The courtyard therefore permits a neck of just one millimetre.

Compare that with the necks you have just drawn. VM arrives from the top of the chip, runs 1.03 mm at 0.30 mm, and widens right on the courtyard boundary, so the courtyard covers it with nothing to spare. The six escapes below the chip cannot be that short, because they have to separate before any of them can widen. They run 1.56 to 2.01 mm. Between a third and a half of each one, 0.48 mm on the shortest and 1.01 mm on the longest, lies outside the only region on this board where a Power-class track is permitted to go below 0.80 mm. Despite the fact that the copper is correct, the rule calls it a violation.

A courtyard belongs to the footprint and not to the board. It is drawn to stop neighbouring parts colliding, and, in this case, it happens to fall near the region we want. Another problem with the courtyard is that it can change if someone edits the footprint. What this rule needs is a boundary chosen for this specific board’s escapes, one we can see while we route them. KiCad has an object that is exactly that, called a ‘rule area’. A rule area is a shape you draw on the board and give a name, which a custom rule can then aim at. Take “Add Rule Area” from the right-hand toolbar, draw a rectangle over U1 and its escapes, and in the properties dialogue that opens name it U1_breakout, tick F.Cu (and nothing else), and leave every one of the five “Keep out” boxes clear (Figure 8.34). A rule area with no keepouts ticked keeps nothing out and changes nothing about the board. It exists only as a named region for a rule to point at, and that is all this one is for.

Figure 8.34: The rule area being drawn. The name is what matters most in this dialogue, because we use that name in a custom rule. intersectsArea is tested in exactly the same way as intersectsCourtyard, against the track’s copper and on whole segments, so the change does not make the test any sharper. It fixes the part you can do something about, which is where the boundary sits. It is now a rectangle you drew for this board’s escapes, and not a footprint property that follows U1 around and changes whenever someone edits it. If a Power-class net has to change layer, give it two vias. No net on this board needed to, and that is what the front-copper plan was aiming at. But should you find a power net that has no route on the front, place a pair of vias side by side. Consult the via-size calculation in the net class section (Figures 8.21 and 8.22). A 0.40 mm barrel with 25 microns of plating through a 1.6 mm board carries 2.31 A at a 10 °C rise. That is less than VM asks of it, and equivalent to only about 0.97 mm of 1 oz track, so a single via under a 1.50 mm run is equivalent to a narrowing of the track. Two vias in parallel fix the issue.

One power net did cross, but the fix is straigh-forward. Rows 3 and 4 are not quite symmetrical. The DRV8833’s bottom row is mirrored about its centre: reading left to right it goes AOUT1, AISEN, AOUT2, then BOUT2, BISEN, BOUT1. Channel A therefore leaves the chip in the same left-to-right order as J2’s two terminals and routes straight down. Channel B does not. If you wire J3 pin 1 to BOUT1 and pin 2 to BOUT2, as the obvious reading of the schematic suggests, then the two nets have to cross somewhere between the chip and the connector, which on a single layer means sending one of them on a long detour around the outside of J3.

A crossing like that can be absorbed in three places, which are the copper, the placement and the schematic. The copper is the expensive one, and here it would have meant about twenty extra millimetres of 1.50 mm track passing underneath a connector body. The placement cannot help either, because J2 and J3 both have to open towards the board edge. That leaves the schematic, and it is the cheapest of the three. Change the wiring so that J3 is wired with BOUT2 on pin 1 and BOUT1 on pin 2, which gives its symbol its pins in the reverse order to J2’s (Figure 8.36). As a result, back in the layout editor, both bridge outputs run straight down. The only cost is that motor B’s two leads are the other way round from motor A’s, so for a DC motor it turns the other way for the same input. Record the reversal on the silkscreen and in the assembly instructions, so that the person wiring the board can see it. It is callout 3 in Figure 8.31, where J3’s pads read BOUT2 then BOUT1 while J2’s read AOUT1 then AOUT2.

Figure 8.36: The change, made in the schematic editor. J2’s pins run 1 then 2 down the page and take AOUT1 then AOUT2, matching the order those nets leave the package in. J3’s run 2 then 1, so BOUT1 from pin 7 lands on terminal 2 and BOUT2 from pin 5 lands on terminal 1, and this keeps the two wires from crossing each other in the layout editor.

Generally, a connector’s pin order is one of the easiest things on a board to change and one of the most expensive to work around in copper, so when a route needs a detour, check the connector before you accept the detour. Make the change in the schematic, re-run “Update PCB from Schematic”, and the ratsnest tells you at once whether the crossing has gone.

AISEN and BISEN are row 5, at the Sense class width of 0.60 mm, and they should be routed in this pass, because they carry motor current. They are short runs from pins 3 and 6 across to R2 and R3 sitting just below, and they escape through the same crowded row as the four bridge outputs, so they have to be drawn while there is still room to draw them.

They need the same treatment on the way in as the power nets do. So each one leaves the pad at 0.30 mm, holds that for a little under two millimetres while it gets clear of its neighbours, and steps up to 0.60 mm for the rest of the way (Figure 8.33). The “U1 fine-pitch breakout” area covers the narrow part exactly as it covers the power nets, and the 0.60 mm section beyond it is wide enough that no rule has anything to say about it.

Look at the clearance on the way out. AISEN runs down between AOUT1 and AOUT2, both of them Power class at 0.30 mm clearance, and on the first attempt at this board it had only 0.44 mm of room to widen into where it needed 0.60. Nudging AOUT1 a tenth of a millimetre out of the way was the fix. A fanout16 like this runs out of room at the widening step, not at the escape itself.

These two nets are on neither of the other two classes. Default at 0.25 mm carries about 0.86 A at the temperature rise this board designs to, and these nets see the full bridge current of 1.5 A. Power at 1.50 mm would be wider than the 1206 pads they arrive at. The Sense class exists because neither of the other two describes what these two nets do.

VCP and VINT are the two shortest connections on the board, from pins 11 and 14 up to C3 and C4 sitting directly above them, and they are the first place where the back layer is useful. The gap between the capacitor row and U1’s top pin row is not empty. The 1.50 mm VM trunk runs along it, on its way from Q1 to pin 12, and both of these nets have to get past it.

There is no way round on the front. VM is Power class at 0.30 mm clearance and it is the widest copper on the board, so there is no gap to squeeze a logic track through between it and the pads it passes. Each of these two nets drops to the back through a via, crosses beneath the trunk, and comes back up. If your own board does the same, nothing has gone wrong. If you find yourself routing either of them the long way round the outside of C3, stop and use the layer you have.

Everything left conveys only a few milliamps. The four direction inputs, nSLEEP, nFAULT, VLOGIC and GATE are all on Default at 0.25 mm (Figure 8.37). They are the easy ones, and that why we are leaving them last.

Copper zones and thermal relief

GND is the last net left. This net carries the motor current back to the power supply. On a signal board a ground pour is largely a convenience. Here it does two jobs at once: it is the return path for up to 3 A, and it is the copper that the heat leaving U1 has to move into.

We will create two zones. The first is the regular ground pour, which fills both layers. The second is a small fill that sits over U1’s ground pads, and exists to overrule the first one there:

Zone Name Layers Net Priority Pad connection
Main ground pour GND F.Cu and B.Cu GND 0 thermal relief, 0.3 mm spokes
Over U1’s ground pads U1-GND-pads F.Cu and B.Cu GND 1 solid, no spokes

Name them as you draw them. A zone’s name is optional, but the design rules check reports violations against zones by name, and therefore these names will help you identify the source of a violation if one is reported by the DRC.

Draw the main pour as a single object. Take the “Add Filled Zone” tool from the right-hand toolbar, and when the properties dialogue opens, tick both F.Cu and B.Cu in the layers list, choose GND as the net, and trace a rectangle that encloses the whole board, running a millimetre or so outside the outline (Figure 8.38).

Drawing the fill outside the board makes this process easier. The fill is clipped by the copper-to-edge clearance rule, which board setup put at 0.5 mm, so the copper stops 0.5 mm short of Edge.Cuts wherever the outline runs and follows the 2 mm filleted corners exactly. Draw the fill rectangle by eye, a millimetre or so clear of the outline the whole way round, and the fill discards the overhang for you. Measure the result and the copper stops 0.5 mm inside the edge to within the fill’s own resolution, on the straights and around the filleted corners alike.

Figure 8.38: One zone object, two layers. F.Cu and B.Cu are both ticked in the layers list. The front and back pours are drawn once, carry one net, and cannot drift apart when a setting changes later. Follow the arrow to the top-left corner of the board, because the outline it points at is the zone’s and not the board’s. Pay attention to the four settings in that dialogue. Leave “Pad connections” on “Thermal reliefs” with 0.3 mm spokes, the hand-soldering choice and the right default for every ordinary ground pad on this board. Leave “Minimum width” at 0.2 mm, the narrowest neck of copper the fill may keep, so that anything thinner is dropped as the zone fills. Set “Remove islands” to “Always”, so that any piece of the fill which reaches no GND pad is deleted instead of left floating. And leave the zone’s own clearance where the class puts it. Remember that GND matches the *GND pattern and is therefore in the Power class, so it carries that class’s 0.30 mm and not the 0.20 mm the logic nets use. Leaving it alone shows up in the dialogue as a clearance of 0, which reads alarmingly and means the opposite of what it looks like. It is not a zone allowed to touch its neighbours. It is a zone stating no preference, and where a zone and its net class disagree, KiCad uses the larger of the two.

Now fill it. Press “B”, and then go and look at U1.

The problem: thermal reliefs starve the pads that need copper most

A thermal relief does not connect a pad to a pour. It connects a pad to a pour through four narrow bridges of copper, and cuts away everything else within the relief gap. On an ordinary ground pad that is the right trade-off. A pad connected to a large pour cannot be heated enough to solder, because the pour carries the heat away as fast as an iron delivers it and the joint never reaches temperature. Four 0.3 mm spokes, though, let the pad heat up while still connecting it electrically, so hand-soldering is possible.

At U1 that trade is not what we want (Figure 8.39). U1’s ground is not a single pad. It is the exposed pad, 3.4 by 5.0 mm on the front and 2.8 by 4.4 mm on the back, plus the twelve plated vias through it. There are fourteen separate pads all carrying the same pad number and the same net.

The footprint you drew in Chapter 2 already uses a solid connection on the exposed pad itself. It does not mandate for solid connections on the twelve vias, because those are separate pads and they were left to inherit from the board. This is the part I did not anticipate. The pour hands each of the twelve its own 0.5 mm relief gap, and at a 1.27 mm pitch those gaps are wider than the space between them. They merge into one scalloped cut that runs right round the outside of the pad, and the copper inside is left attached at its four corners only. The exposed pad got the solid connection it required, and then twelve small holes cut it off from the plane anyway.

Figure 8.39: The problem, with the front copper switched off so that the back plane can be seen. The rectangle in the middle is U1’s back-side heatsink pad, solidly connected as per the footprint design. Around it, the twelve relief gaps belonging to the twelve vias have merged into one continuous cut, and the arrows mark the four corners where the copper inside is still attached to the plane outside it. This is what thermal relief does to a via field, as opposed to a single pad.

Two things are wrong with this situation.

  • Heat. That pad is the only route out of the die. Recall the numbers from the section on sizing the net class. The DRV8833 is rated at 1.5 A per bridge in this package and 500 mA in the TSSOP, which is the same silicon without an exposed pad. That extra ampere per bridge comes from the exposed pad, and the pad delivers it only when it is connected to copper that can carry the heat away.
  • Current. The exposed pad is also the star point, the single node where every motor return current converges before it reaches the ground pour. Up to 3 A comes back through it, and the four corner bridges cannot carry that current.

The solution: a second zone that fills solid, at a higher priority

The solution is to keep thermal reliefs everywhere else on this board, because they are appropriate for hand-soldering, but overrule them at U1 only. To achieve this, we will introduce a second zone on the same net that covers U1’s ground pads and fills solid.

The KiCad mechanism that will help us here is zone priority. Two zones on the same layer are filled in priority order, highest first, and a lower-priority zone keeps clearance to what the higher one has already filled. So a small high-priority zone drawn over the pads behaves as a local override. Inside its outline its own settings decide, and the main pour fills around it.

Instead of using a zone, we could set each of the vias to Solid by hand, but this would require twelve separate edits. Drawing one shape that instructs all contained vias to use solid connections catches all twelve at once.

You should keep in mind that the solid connection makes the pad harder to hand-solder. Three things make it acceptable here. First, the datasheet is unambiguous that the pad must be soldered to the board for the part to reach its rating. A pad you can solder easily but which does not conduct heat is a failure anyway. In this case, and in general, the quality of the assembly is more important than convenience to hand-solder. Second, the solder mask window over that pad is 2.46 mm by 2.31 mm against 3.4 mm by 5.0 mm of copper beneath it, which is how you drew it in Chapter 2, so the joint you are actually making is smaller than the pad looks. And, third, Chapter 11, Assembling the Board: Reflow or Hand Soldering, offers a route that does not need spokes at all. It involves flooding the pad from the underside through the via field, with the board the other way up.

Drawing the override zone and giving it priority

We will draw the override zone in four steps: draw the zone over U1’s ground pads, set it to fill solid, raise it above the main pour, and refill the board.

Here are the details:

  1. Draw the zone. Take “Add Filled Zone” again and trace a rectangle over U1’s exposed pad and its whole via field. On this board that is 6.1 by 4.0 mm, which is the pad plus about a millimetre all round. Size it to cover the pads you want to change and nothing else. As you can see, pad 13 is GND, in the middle of the top pin row, with pin 12 carrying VM on one side and pin 14 carrying VINT on the other, both 0.20 mm away and neither of them ground. The pour has no way in from either side, because the copper it would need to get there belongs to two other nets. It can reach the pad from the outer end and nowhere else, which is one spoke, and no amount of refilling will find a second. So, pad 13 is left with a single spoke.

A via of its own is not possible either. There is no room for one inside a 0.65 mm pad row, the same constraint that forced every neck-down in the routing section. So the pad was tied instead by a 0.30 mm track running straight down into the exposed pad, the best ground on this board.

We now have a situation where the pad is well-connected but the check fails. The design rule requiring pads to have at least two spokes is a proxy that works well in most cases. Here, though, the proxy and the intention behind it have come apart. The pad is attached better than two spokes would have attached it, but this route cannot be assessed properly by the spoke count rule.

There are three things you can do about a violation you have decided is not a defect.

  1. Exclude the marker. Right-click it and choose to exclude it. This is the quickest, but the worst. I admit that it’s the one I have used most often, because it’s just a click. Excluding the violation hides the problem instead of solving it. The violation disappears from the list and the reason for excluding it is recorded nowhere at all, so the next person to open this board, including you in a year, sees a clean DRC and never learns that a decision was made.
  2. Write the exception down as a custom rule. A rule states the condition it applies to, and the comment above it states the reasoning, in the file that travels with the project. This is normally the right answer, and it is the one this chapter reached for first. On current KiCad it is unavailable, for a reason the subsection below sets out.
  3. Change the board so the check passes. Not always possible, but it is the best of the three.

The fix in this case is to change a setting specific to pad 13. The check is about thermal relief, so stop asking for thermal relief on that pad. Double-click pad 13 to open Pad Properties, go to the Connections tab, and under “Connection to Copper Zones” change “Pad connection” from “From parent footprint” to Solid (Figure 8.49). Refill, and the error is gone.

Figure 8.49: One pad, configured to behave differently from the rest. The control is “Pad connection”. It is on the Connections tab, under the “Connection to Copper Zones” group heading. Set “Pad connection” to Solid.

That is not a way of silencing the check. It is the same decision the last section made at the exposed pad, applied to the other ground pin, and it adds copper instead of hiding the absence of it. The single 0.3 mm spoke is replaced by the pad’s whole outer end merging into the pour. The check stops firing because there is nothing left to complain about.

Remember, not all pads are the same. This is the more useful lesson, so take it out of the specific case. A footprint arrives looking like one indivisible object, sixteen pads and an exposed pad that you place, rotate and route as a unit. But in reality, it is not indivisible. Every pad in it is an object with its own properties, and almost all of them can be set for that one pad and nothing else.

The dialogue in Figure 8.49 demonstrates this idea. Find the “Pad connection” dropdown. Next to it sits “Thermal Relief Overrides”, where relief gap, spoke width and spoke angle are left blank on this pad. Blank does not mean zero; it means inherit. A pad can take its relief geometry from the zone that fills it, or it can state its own and ignore the zone entirely. The Clearance Overrides tab next door does the same for clearance, mask and paste.

You have already used this earlier on this board without naming it. U1’s exposed pad carries a Solid connection because the footprint you drew in Chapter 2 set it on that pad. The twelve thermal vias deliberately do not, and that is why the pour gave them reliefs and cut the pad off, and why a zone could catch them. Pad 13 now carries its own. Three pads on one chip, three different answers, each appropriate on a case-by-case basis.

The order of precedence runs the opposite way to how people usually assume, so here it is plainly:

Level Where it is set Default
Pad “Pad connection”, Connections tab of Pad Properties From parent footprint
Footprint “Pad connection to zones”, Pad Connections tab of Footprint Properties From zone setting
Zone “Pad connections”, Copper Zone Properties the mode itself

Read that table from the top down. The pad’s own setting wins because it has a higher priority. The footprint’s setting applies only to pads left on From parent footprint, and the zone’s setting applies only where the footprint in turn is left on From zone setting. A zone never overrules a pad that has stated its own connection mode; it supplies the setting for pads that have not stated one. That is why the U1-GND-pads zone was able to change the twelve thermal vias. Each via was left on From parent footprint, the footprint left them on From zone setting, and so the zone was the first level in the chain that had a setting of its own to apply.

Treating pads differently is the feature that you can use when differentiation is an appropriate answer to a design problem.

There is an obvious way to record this decision in the project itself, which is a custom rule that tells the DRC to accept one spoke on this pad. On the versions of KiCad this book was written against, that rule cannot be used, for a reason that has nothing to do with the rule. The aside below has the detail and a way to test whether your own version still suffers from it.

A bug: a spoke rule can disable your clearance rules. The rule you would want to write is this one, and it looks entirely reasonable:

(rule "U1 pad 13 reaches ground by track"
  (constraint min_resolved_spokes (min 1))
  (condition "A.memberOfFootprint('U1') && A.Pad_Number == '13'")
)

Adding any min_resolved_spokes constraint to a .kicad_dru stops every custom clearance constraint in that file from being applied, board-wide. Here the pad-13 error clears and the “U1 fine-pitch breakout” relaxation dies with it, so all six escapes fall back to the Power class’s 0.30 mm and the board goes from one error to thirty-eight. Only clearance is affected; track_width and silk_clearance go on working. Nothing warns you, and, even worse, the failure looks like success. I confirmed it on the releases current as I write, 10.0.5 and 10.0.6, down to a spoke rule whose condition matches nothing on the board at all, and it is a bug I have not seen reported.

Test your own version on a throwaway copy of the project. Raise the “U1 fine-pitch breakout” clearance from 0.15 mm to something large, say 0.80 mm, and run the DRC. You should get a flood of clearance violations, which proves the custom constraint is being applied. Now add the spoke rule above and run it again. If the flood remains, your version is fixed and you can use the rule. If the count collapses, and the violations that remain name the netclass rather than the rule, the bug is still there.

A rules file that parses is not the same as a rules file that works. After any change to your custom rules, re-run DRC and confirm that the violations you expected to keep are still being reported. A rule that quietly disables a check is worse than no rule.

Completing the Bill of Materials

Chapter 5’s field-name templates are already in this project. For this board, I will add a fourth, Manufacturer. If you don’t remember how to do this, refer to Chapter 5, Bill of Materials, In Depth, and its section on field-name templates.

Let’s look at what these fields mean:

  • MPN holds the manufacturer’s part number, as the manufacturer prints it.
  • Manufacturer holds the company that makes the part. A part number is very nearly unique on its own, but there is no guarantee that two manufacturers are not using the same part number for very different products. In such cases, the manufacturer name will help resolve the conflict.
  • Supplier holds the distributor you buy it from, by name. The parts on the same board can come from different suppliers. A bill of twenty parts frequently comes from two or three places. If one distributor happens to stock everything, order from that one, as it is the simplest and often the cheapest option.
  • SupplierURL holds the link to that distributor’s page for that part.

For U1, the “Supplier” field currently reads https://www.ti.com/product/DRV8833, a link and not a name, pointing at the manufacturer and not at a distributor. That was a reasonable thing to type in Chapter 1, when the field was the only place to put a useful address. It is the wrong thing now that four fields exist. Put the distributor’s name in “Supplier”, move the address into “SupplierURL”, and point it at the distributor’s page.

Where to find part numbers

There are two ways to find the part number of any part.

The first is by hand, and it is the most reliable. You only need a browser. Open a distributor’s parametric search, choose the type of component, and then filter it down with the package, the value, the tolerance, the voltage or power rating, and the dielectric if it is a ceramic capacitor. What is left is a short list, from where you copy the manufacturer’s part number off whichever line you like. digikey.com and mouser.com both search their own catalogues this way. oemsecrets.com searches across a large number of distributors at once, which is the quickest way to find who has stock of a part you have already settled on. None of this is difficult. But it is slow, because it is a manual and serial method. That slowness is why so many projects reach a factory with the sourcing fields still empty.

The second way is to hand the repetition to a search engine built for it. I wrote a web application called BomExplorer, at bomexplorer.app, because I was tired of doing this by hand on every board. It takes a bill of materials as a file, from KiCad or from any other tool, looks each line up against online parts databases, and returns a manufacturer, a part number, the source it found the match through and a unit price for every row it can match (Figure 8.58). You get the file it needs from the same Symbol Fields Table. The “Export” tab writes the CSV BomExplorer reads. On your own boards, BomExplorer makes sourcing much faster.

One project, two boards

Chapter 5 has already discussed this board’s variant, and focused on the R2 and R3 resistors that make up the difference between the variants. Here is a quick recap. The “without-sense” build changes what R2 and R3 are rather than whether they are fitted, because those two resistors are the path the motor current takes back to ground and a bridge without one cannot conduct at all. If that reasoning is not fresh, read the section on design variants and DNP in Chapter 5, Bill of Materials, In Depth, again before you touch anything.

What is left is to build it. Open the variant dropdown in the schematic editor’s top toolbar, choose “Add New Design Variant”, and name it without-sense. This creates an exact copy of the base design with nothing overridden. Then select it in the “Schematic variants” list at the foot of the Symbol Fields Table’s left panel and change three cells on the R2, R3 row:

Value         0R2                    →  0R
MPN           CSR1206FKR200          →  CRCW12060000Z0EA
Manufacturer  Stackpole Electronics  →  Vishay

Change all three cells, in one go. Chapter 5 warned that a variant will allow you to change a value and forget the part number, which is a costly mistake. If you made this mistake, the assembler would be required to purchase a 0.2 Ω sense resistor and fit it where a 0 Ω link belongs. The manufacturer value is the easiest of the three to leave behind.

A jumper (0 Ω resistor) has a current rating like any other component, and this one is carrying a whole bridge’s motor current, against the DRV8833’s 1.5 A continuous and 2 A peak per bridge. The part I have chosen, CRCW12060000Z0EA, is rated 2 A. The common 0603 jumper is rated 1 A, which is fine on a signal net but not here. Note also that it is a 1206 part, because R2 and R3 are 1206 on this board. A variant may change a footprint, but changing this one would mean two different boards instead of two builds of one, so the substitute has to fit the land pattern that is already on the copper.

How to check that the override is live

Use the grouping you set earlier as a check for the override (Figure 8.59).

“MPN” is a column in the bill, but it is not one of the fields you grouped by. “Value” is. So, R2 and R3 stay on one row as long as they agree about the value that groups them, and that one row has a single cell in which to say what two symbols carry. If you change the value on both, but forget the new part number on one, that cell then has to report a mismatch between the two members of the group. KiCad neither splits the row nor picks a winner. Instead, it writes -- mixed values -- where the part number should be, and the same again in “Manufacturer” beside it (Figure 8.60).

Make that warning appear once on purpose, so that you know what it looks like when it happens for real.

  1. Untick “Group symbols” at the top of the dialogue. R2 and R3 now have a row each.
  2. Set “Value” to 0R on both rows. Then set “MPN” and “Manufacturer” on the R3 row only, and leave R2’s two cells alone.
  3. Tick “Group symbols” again. The two are one row once more, because they still agree about the value that groups them, and the two cells beside it now read -- mixed values -- (Figure 8.60).
  4. Click the triangle at the left of the row to open the group. R3 carries CRCW12060000Z0EA and R2 does not, which is how you tell which half is unfinished.
  5. Fill in R2’s two cells. Both summary cells give way to the values they should have had.

Grouping is a view setting, so selecting and deselecting does not change anything in the schematic. It does change the exported bill, though, because the export writes the table as it stands at the time.

The exported file does not repeat the warning, and that is why this check belongs in the dialogue. -- mixed values -- is drawn on the screen, and you will not see it in the exported file. If you export the bill in the state the figure shows, the R2, R3 row comes out reading CRCW12060000Z0EA against a quantity of 2. Here that happens to be the part you wanted on both resistors, so the file is right by luck. R2 still carries no part number of its own, and the bill has filled the silence from its neighbour. If you leave R2 holding its base number instead of blanking it, then the same row exports as CRCW12060000Z0EA,CSR1206FKR200, showing two part numbers joined into one string that does not match anything real.

In summary, a cell reading -- mixed values -- when you believe the variant is finished means the override reached one symbol of the group and not the other. Nothing else will tell you. Specifying the surface finish: HASL or ENIG

Go back to “Board Setup”, then “Physical Stackup”, and look at the field that says what finish goes on the exposed copper. On this project it is still empty.

Bare copper corrodes. A ‘surface finish’ is what the factory puts on the pads to stop that happening between fabrication and assembly. It is one of the few genuine choices you make about how the board is built, rather than about what is on it. HASL17, which is hot air solder levelling, dips the board in molten solder and blows the excess off. It is cheap, robust on the shelf, and leaves pads that are slightly domed. ENIG18, which is electroless nickel immersion gold, plates a thin nickel layer and a thinner gold one, costs more, and leaves pads that are genuinely flat.

On this board the ENIG option is the better one (Figure 8.62). Let’s look at it more closely. A 0.65 mm pitch package with sixteen gull-wing leads and a 3.4 by 5.0 mm thermal pad sits better on flat pads. A slight dome on a 0.45 mm land makes a lead sit less evenly than it could, and an uneven surface across a pad the size of the thermal pad is one of the things that lets a chip tilt on its paste. In the section drawn in Figure 8.62, the three HASL feet come to rest at three different heights, 8 µm between the highest and the lowest, while the three ENIG feet rest at one.

The two halves of that figure are labelled differently, and the reason is worth knowing. IPC-4552B sets how thick an ENIG finish has to be, 3 to 6 µm of nickel under 0.05 to 0.10 µm of gold, so a board ordered with that finish arrives inside a known band. No standard sets a thickness for HASL at all. J-STD-003 governs whether a finish solders, not how thick it is or how flat it stays, which is why the domes in the figure are one plausible board in the 10 to 25 µm region ordinary practice produces rather than a number you can order to.

Plenty of boards carrying this package are built on HASL and work perfectly well, so this is a preference and not a requirement. I would specify ENIG here. Set it on the “Physical Stackup” page, where it travels with the project.

Figure 8.62: A section across three of U1’s lead lands, cut in the same place and finished two ways, HASL above and ENIG below. It is true to scale across the 0.65 mm pitch but stretched six times vertically, so use the 0.035 mm copper foil as your ruler for the finish sitting on top of it.

Paste coverage and the vias under the exposed pad

In Chapter 2 you split the paste opening under U1 in two, and you cleared every technical layer off the twelve thermal vias. Both decisions were made on the footprint, one object at a time, before there was a board to see them on. Figure 8.63 draws them together on the finished design.

This is a solder-mask-defined pad, so the mask window decides how much of the pad the solder can reach. In Figure 8.63, that window sits well inside the copper, and the two paste apertures sit inside the window. Together they cover roughly two thirds of the window, inside the 50 to 80 per cent band that is normal practice on a thermal pad. If you make this mask smaller, the joint is starved. If you make it as large as the window, the chip will float on the molten solder. The gap in the middle does a second job. As the paste collapses at reflow, the flux boiling out of it needs a route to the edge, and giving it one keeps voids out of a joint this large.

Paste has to let go of the aperture walls when the foil lifts, and IPC-752519 gives you two ratios for checking that it will. Each one is a number you work out from the aperture and the foil, then compare against a minimum. The first is the ‘area ratio’. This is the area of the opening divided by the area of the four walls around it. Those two areas stand for two competing forces. The pad below pulls the paste down over the area it touches, and the walls hold it back over the area they touch. A wide, shallow aperture has much more floor than wall. A small aperture cut in thick foil has more wall than floor, and the paste clings to those walls and stays behind. The second is the ‘aspect ratio’. This is the aperture’s narrow dimension divided by the thickness of the foil. It catches a long, narrow slot, where the area ratio can pass while the opening is still too slender for the foil it is cut in.

Falling below either minimum predicts a bad print, not a bad joint. Part of the paste stays in the stencil when the foil lifts, and the pad receives less than the aperture was drawn to deliver. What you see when the board comes off the printer is a thin or ragged deposit instead of a full one. Later, at reflow, that joint is starved. These are wide apertures in a thin foil, and the footer line under the figure shows both ratios several times clear of their limits.

Texas Instruments, in its datasheet, specifies a single opening, and shrinks it as the stencil foil gets thicker, so that the volume of paste stays roughly the same whichever foil your assembler uses. The table is on page 28 of the DRV8833 datasheet, on the sheet headed “EXAMPLE STENCIL DESIGN” for package PWP0016C, immediately after the land pattern drawing you worked from in Chapter 2. It gives one aperture per foil thickness: 2.75 by 2.58 mm for a 0.1 mm foil, 2.46 by 2.31 mm for 0.125 mm, 2.25 by 2.11 mm for 0.15 mm, and 2.08 by 1.95 mm for 0.175 mm. Multiply any row out and the deposit comes to the same 0.71 mm³, which is the point of the table. The second row is a number you have already met, because 2.46 by 2.31 mm is the solder mask window in Figure 8.63, so at the foil this board uses TI’s single aperture fills that window edge to edge. Volume is what both approaches manage. Splitting the opening holds it down as well, and it gives the flux its escape route. Both are accepted practice.

None of the twelve vias carries a mask layer of its own, because you cleared those layers when you drew them. For the six in the outer rows that is enough. These fall outside the mask window, so the mask runs over them, and they are tented. The six inner ones fall inside the window, so the mask opens over them. Four sit under a paste aperture, and the last two sit on the centre line, straddling the gap and touching both. The key beside the figure names all three states, so you can check each barrel for yourself.

TI’s own land pattern drawing has a note about this, note 10 at the foot of page 27 of the DRV8833 datasheet, on the “EXAMPLE BOARD LAYOUT” sheet for PWP0016C. You have that page in front of you already, because Chapter 2 reproduces it whole, notes and all, at Figure 2.15. The note reads “Vias are optional depending on application, refer to device data sheet. It is recommended that vias under paste be filled, plugged or tented.” The reason is capillary action, the same effect that draws molten solder up a lead. A barrel with an open mouth in the middle of a joint pulls solder down into itself at reflow. The solder does not escape underneath, because the back of this board is masked from edge to edge, but solder that goes in the hole has still left the joint. Remember, this joint carries every watt of heat the chip produces.

So this board does not follow the datasheet’s recommendation, as a result of deliberate choice. Those six openings are the only places where a hand-built joint touches the top face of the pad, because the underside carries no bare metal to solder to and the iron has to fill the barrels from below. If you close the six openings, the reflowed joint improves, but the second way of building this board is no longer possible. This part was chosen because it can be built both ways, and a better reflowed joint does not justify losing one of those two methods. Route two, in Chapter 11, Assembling the Board: Reflow or Hand Soldering, describes the hand method in full.

If you are having this board reflowed, you have two ways to close the gap:

  1. Ask the fabricator to plug the vias. Via plugging is a standard process option, it is normally quoted per board, and it fills the barrels with resin before the surface finish goes on. Say which holes you mean specifically instead of asking for plugged vias in general. Those twelve holes are declared as component drills, not via drills, because in your footprint they are pads of U1 and not separate vias on the board. A blanket “plug all vias” instruction might have unintended consequences.
  2. Accept the reduced joint. On a board driving small motors, with a 1 A chopping limit and a ground plane under the chip, a thermal joint that is not fully wetted is not a failure. It is simply a smaller margin than the datasheet assumes, and you should consider documenting it on the User.Comments layer beside the note about pad 13.

  1. A P-channel MOSFET is a three-terminal semiconductor switch; MOSFET stands for metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor. The voltage between the gate and source terminals controls whether current can flow between the other two terminals, the drain and the source, and in the P-channel variety the device turns on when the gate sits below the source. That suits it to the positive side of a supply line, exactly where this one sits. Fully on, it behaves like a small resistor of a few tens of milliohms, which lets it carry the motor current with very little loss.↩︎

  2. A bypass capacitor is a small capacitor placed between a chip’s supply pin and ground, as close to the pin as the board allows. It keeps a local store of charge that the chip can draw on the instant its current demand jumps, keeping the pin’s voltage steady instead of letting it dip while the extra current travels in along the board’s tracks. The same part is also called a decoupling capacitor, and both names appear in this book.↩︎

  3. A current-sense resistor is a resistor of deliberately low value placed in the path of the current it is meant to measure. Ohm’s law gives a voltage across it proportional to the current passing through, and the circuit reads that small voltage to learn how much current is flowing. On this board R2 and R3 sit in the motor current’s path back to ground, each just 0.2 Ω, and the DRV8833 uses the drop across them to enforce a 1 A chopping limit on the motor current.↩︎

  4. Reflow is the soldering process that assembles a surface-mount board. Solder paste is printed onto the pads through a stencil, the components are placed on top, and the whole board is then heated until the paste melts and flows into the joints, which set as it cools. Chapter 11, Assembling the Board: Reflow or Hand Soldering, builds this board that way, and also the other way, with an iron.↩︎

  5. An open-drain output has a single transistor inside the chip, connected between the output pin and ground, with no connection to a positive supply. When the transistor is on, it pulls the line down to ground, and when it is off, it leaves the line unconnected, or ‘open’; the name comes from the transistor’s drain terminal being left this way. Because the pin can only pull down and never drive up, the line needs a pull-up resistor to a supply rail to reach a logic high. That pull-down-only behaviour is also what lets several open-drain outputs share a single line and a single resistor, as Chapter 9 does with its two fault signals.↩︎

  6. Derating is the practice of running a part below its rated maximum, keeping spare capacity for conditions worse than those the rating was measured under. A resistor’s power rating is stated at a particular ambient temperature, commonly 70 °C, and above that temperature the permitted dissipation falls, reaching zero at the part’s maximum operating temperature. On a board shared with heat sources the resistor runs hotter than the rating assumes, so its true allowance is smaller than the number in the datasheet.↩︎

  7. A trip voltage is the fixed threshold at which a protection or control circuit acts, borrowing the same word as a fuse or circuit breaker that ‘trips’ when its limit is reached. Inside the DRV8833 a comparator watches the voltage on the sense pin, and the moment it reaches the trip voltage the chip switches the bridge so the motor current falls back. The chip then repeats this cycle as often as needed to hold the current at its limit, and that repeated switching is the ‘chopping’ in the chopping limit.↩︎

  8. To ‘bias’ a diode is to put a voltage across it, and the bias is called forward when the voltage pushes in the diode’s conducting direction, so that current flows with only a small drop of a few hundred millivolts. The opposite is reverse bias, where the voltage pushes the other way and the diode blocks, passing nothing but a tiny leakage current. This paragraph walks the body diode through both states, one for each way the supply can be connected.↩︎

  9. A clamp is a circuit that puts a ceiling on a voltage, holding the node it protects at or below a set level no matter what the rest of the circuit does. The usual part for the job is a Zener diode, which conducts once the voltage across it reaches its rated value, diverting the excess current and stopping the voltage from rising further. Here the clamp in question would be a Zener across Q1’s gate and source, limiting the voltage the gate oxide ever sees.↩︎

  10. A neck-down is a deliberate local narrowing of a track, made so that a wide run can reach a pad, or pass through a gap, narrower than the run itself. The track returns to its full width once it is clear of the obstruction, and because the narrowed section still carries the whole current of the run, a neck-down is kept as short as the geometry allows.↩︎

  11. IPC-2221 is the Generic Standard on Printed Board Design, published by IPC, the trade association whose standards most of the board industry works to. It is the document behind both of the calculator tabs this section relies on: the arithmetic relating a conductor’s cross-section to the current it can carry, which sized the Power class a few paragraphs ago and which KiCad prints as a formula under the “Track Width” panels, and the spacing table you are looking at here. Consulting it is not a formality, because it answers a question you cannot settle by eye, which is whether the voltage on your board is high enough to be the thing that sets your clearance. On this board the answer is no, which is what leaves clearance free to be a manufacturing decision. On a mains-adjacent board the same table would produce the largest number in Board Setup. You do not need a copy of the standard to work this way, since KiCad ships the parts of it a board like this one needs.↩︎

  12. Ampacity is how much current a conductor can carry continuously without heating itself more than a stated amount above its surroundings. The temperature rise belongs to the figure: 2.31 A here means 2.31 A at a 10 °C rise, and the same via allowed 20 °C would come back with a larger number. The limit is thermal and not electrical, so a conductor pushed past its ampacity does not stop conducting and trips nothing; it simply runs hotter than the design assumed. On a board the figure follows from the copper’s cross-section, which is why a track and a via can be compared with the same arithmetic.↩︎

  13. The two panels do not use the same copper thickness, which is worth knowing before you check this arithmetic by hand. Entered as 1 oz/ft², KiCad takes external copper to be 34.4 microns, while the “Internal Layer Tracks” panel below carries its own thickness field, set here to 35 microns. Divide 0.0334 mm² by 34.4 microns and you get the 0.97 mm on screen; divide it by 35 and you get 0.95 mm. The difference is immaterial to any decision on this board, but it explains why two panels answering the same question return slightly different widths.↩︎

  14. The grid origin is the zero point that the grid is laid out from. Move it, and every grid dot moves with it. It is also the reference that “Position Relative To…” counts typed positions from. The page origin is the other, fixed zero, at the sheet’s top-left corner, and is what the coordinate readout measures from until you change it.   15. To scope a rule is to limit which objects it applies to. In KiCad the scope is the rule’s condition clause, where A names the object DRC is testing. The two rules above are scoped in two different ways: “Power minimum width” selects by net class, and the breakout rule selects by geometry.   16. A fanout is the pattern of short copper where the nets leaving a dense pad group separate from one another. Each track leaves its pad at the width the pitch allows, runs out parallel to its neighbours, then steps up to the width the rest of the board uses. The individual pieces already have names here, an escape for the hop off the pad and a ‘neck-down’ for the change of width, but the fanout is the whole pattern and the room it needs.↩︎

  17. The name comes from the air knives rather than the dip: after the masked board passes over a bath of molten solder, jets of hot air level the surplus off the pads to an even thickness. What is left behind is a thin layer of solder itself, and that layer, not the copper underneath, is the surface the component leads will solder to.↩︎

  18. Both halves of the name describe processes that run without an electrical current. The nickel is plated by a chemical reaction on the board’s own surface, which is what ‘electroless’ means, and it forms a layer a few microns thick. The gold is picked up by simply dipping the board in a gold solution, ‘immersion’: gold atoms take the place of the outer shell of the nickel, and because nothing is driven by a current, only a skin a fraction of a micron thick can build. The solder does its work on the nickel. At reflow, it wets the nickel and forms its joint with it, while the gold sits on top as a corrosion-proof skin. That is also why the pads finish flat. Two smooth plating layers sit where a levelled solder layer would dome.↩︎

  19. IPC-7525 is Stencil Design Guidelines, published by IPC, the same body behind the IPC-2221 spacing table earlier in this chapter. Both ratios are defined in its “Terms and Definitions” section, at 1.8.2 and 1.8.3 on page 2, and the section that sets the limits is 3.2.1.2, “Area Ratio/Aspect Ratio”, on page 5. IPC sells it at https://shop.ipc.org/ipc-7525/ipc-7525-standard-only/Revision-c/english, and there is no public copy to read, so the 0.66 and 1.5 in the footer of Figure 8.63 are the values as revision B states them, which is the form the industry quotes.   20. Excellon is a company name rather than a standard. Excellon Automation Co. built the drilling and routing machines that led the market in the 1980s and still trades today, and the command language its machines read became the industry’s default drill format by being copied, not by being ratified. The one formal standard here is IPC-NC-349 (1985), which the Excellon Format 2 that KiCad writes exceeds rather than follows, and the vendor’s own specification is no longer published. That history is why the format is ambiguous about coordinates and units, which is the thing the “Zeros” setting two sentences from here has to settle.↩︎

  21. Frameless describes how the stencil reaches you, not something about the stencil itself. A stencil is a thin stainless-steel foil with one aperture cut over every paste pad, and a supplier sends it in one of two forms. A frameless stencil is the bare foil, usually a sheet a few hundred millimetres across and much larger than the board it serves. A framed one has that same foil stretched flat and clamped into an aluminium frame, which is what a paste printer expects. The frame locates in the machine, and the tension keeps the foil from lifting or smearing while the ‘squeegee’ drags paste across it. Framing adds a part and an assembly step on top of the same cut foil, so the surcharge for it often costs more than the foil itself. When you print by hand you provide the flat backing and the clamping yourself, so the frame is not needed.↩︎
